# Research Methods

## Lecture 1 — Introduction · Reality and Consensus

### 1.1 What is research?

- **Frascati Manual (OECD, 2015):** R&D is _creative work undertaken on a systematic basis to increase the stock of knowledge_ (including knowledge of humans, culture and society) _and to use it to devise new applications_.
- **Three types of R&D**

  | Type                     | Goal                                                                                     | Application in view?          |
  | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
  | Basic research           | New knowledge of the underlying foundations of phenomena                                 | No                            |
  | Applied research         | New knowledge                                                                            | Yes, a specific practical aim |
  | Experimental development | Uses existing knowledge to produce or substantially improve products, processes, systems | Yes                           |

- **Rashid (Microsoft Research, 2006):** advanced product development is _not_ research. Research must be publishable in a peer-reviewed venue and stand up to the best people in the field.
- **Humanities view:** research is not only discovering unknowns. It also includes **reinterpreting or rediscovering known artifacts** (texts, cultural products). The fundamental point is the **creation of something new**, whether as art (literary authorship) or as scholarly analysis.
- **Oates (2006):** good research does not jump to conclusions. It needs
  - sufficient and appropriate data sources
  - proper recording, analysis and interpretation of the data
  - well-founded conclusions
  - presentation in an acceptable form (report, thesis, paper)
- Research creates new knowledge **to the satisfaction of the user(s) of the research**.

### 1.2 What a researcher does

- Assembles and **defends an argument** that new knowledge has been created, drawing on others' work
- Looks for new abstractions, approaches, algorithms, principles, mechanisms
- **Chooses the activities appropriate** to the questions
- Uses a **variety of approaches and tools**
- Searches for answers even when the precise questions are not yet known

### 1.3 Two key characteristics

- **Rigorous**: a systematic, valid, appropriate process
- **Relevant**: pertinent to someone, society, or the research community

### 1.4 PhD

- The highest academic degree: master a subject and **extend its body of knowledge**
- Leads to research careers in industry or research centres, or academic positions

### 1.5 Truth and credibility (Bruckman, 2005)

- Sociologists of science: **truth is socially constructed**. A hypothesis is asserted by a person or small group, more and more people accept it, and it becomes a "fact".
- Even an objectivist must admit that the _process_ of becoming accepted as fact is social.
- **Peer review = the institutionalisation of the process of creating facts.**
- **Credibility checklist** for any piece of information:
  1. Who asserted this?
  2. What are their credentials?
  3. Who had the opportunity to critique it?
  4. Who supports it, and who disagrees?
- An informal online post can still carry some credibility, depending on its source and the responses to it. Something formatted like an article on a site with a radical agenda may carry none.
- In science the standard is: _"we believe this because these peer-reviewed publications support it."_

### 1.6 Kuhn (1970): paradigms and revolutions

- **Paradigm**: a set of assumptions, concepts, values and practices forming an accepted way of viewing reality, held as true _for a period of time_. It is still only an agreement within a **scientific community**.
- **How paradigms form:** "mere facts" are collected → pre-paradigmatic schools compete → one paradigm gains strength and advocates → it **turns a group into a discipline** (journals, societies) → students learn it to join the community.
- **Scientific revolution:** an **anomaly** (a violation of paradigm-induced expectations) is discovered → professional commitments shift. Example: planetary motion (Copernicus, Kepler, Newton).

### 1.7 Science is negotiated (Tedre et al., 2006)

- Decisions are also driven by economic, political, ideological and cultural motives.
  - GNU/Linux: technical, economic and ideological motivations together
  - OS/2 lost to Windows 95 for non-technical reasons
- ICT is interdisciplinary and open to several paradigms (e.g., ACM SIGs).

### 1.8 Fraud and ethics

- **Fraud** (non-replicable) vs **research bias** (narrowly replicable)
- **Falsification / fabrication**: hiding data that does not support your case; manipulating data
- **Plagiarism**: passing off others' work as your own. It deceives readers and deprives the author of credit.
- Discussion points: **self-plagiarism**; **legal ≠ ethical** (research can be legal but unethical)
- Case: Woo Suk Hwang, fraudulent cloned-embryo stem-cell data (2006)

## Lecture 2 — Information Sources

### 2.1 Recording references

Keep:

1. a brief summary of the content
2. a brief summary of **your evaluation**
3. full bibliographic details, so readers can find the source again

Tools: index cards in the past (Eco), reference-management software today. Software lets you search your notes and insert citations into your word processor.

### 2.2 Referencing when writing

- Relate the critical literature review to **your topic and objectives**.
- Move from general key ideas in the domain → your specific research questions.
- **Prefer concept-structured writing over author-structured writing.**
  - Author-structured: "X (year) found A, Y (year) found B…"
  - Concept-structured (preferred): "Evidence for A has been found in (X, year; Z, year)…"

### 2.3 Citation styles

- **Author–date**: names and year in the text; reference list sorted alphabetically
- **Numeric**: numbers in the text; reference list sorted by number
- The exact format is set by each publication venue.

### 2.4 Ten types of information sources

| #   | Source                       | Is                   | Advantage                    | Disadvantage            | Warning                     |
| --- | ---------------------------- | -------------------- | ---------------------------- | ----------------------- | --------------------------- |
| 1   | Newspaper articles           | Current              | Broad scope                  | Non-substantive         | Check the author            |
| 2   | Magazine articles            | Popular              | Focused                      | Limited coverage        | Check the editorial board   |
| 3   | Books                        | Comprehensive        | Substantive                  | One time, place, author | Check the author            |
| 4   | Manuals                      | Useful               | Detailed description         | Not refereed            | Check for errors            |
| 5   | Reports                      | Appealing            | More problems than solutions | No objectivity          | Check for conceptual errors |
| 6   | Preprints / progress reports | Current              | In step with own research    | Unreliable              | Check for all errors        |
| 7   | Conference proceedings       | Mirror the field now | Variety                      | Heterogeneous quality   | Check quality               |
| 8   | Refereed journals            | Scholarly            | Authoritative                | 1–2 years delay         | —                           |
| 9   | Refereed reviews             | Scholarly            | Authoritative and broad      | Partial scope           | —                           |
| 10  | People                       | Everything           | Deep tacit knowledge         | Hard to find            | —                           |

### 2.5 Role of each source

- Newspapers and magazines → awareness of major changes in related fields
- Reports → problems and demands from industry and society
- Books → learning appealing points of view
- Conference proceedings → **trends** in your community
- Refereed journals → **foundations** for your own research
- Manuals → everyday tasks
- Reviews → **starting a new line of work**

Judgement in using sources is developed over time.

## Lecture 3 — Research Methods Overview

### 3.1 Scientific method

There is no single universal method, but it usually includes:

1. define the problem
2. gather background information
3. form a hypothesis
4. make observations
5. test the hypothesis
6. draw conclusions
7. report results

### 3.2 Related methods (Zelkowitz & Wallace, 1998)

- **Scientific:** develop a theory → hypothesis → test variations → data verify or refute it
- **Engineering:** develop and test a solution → improve until no further improvement is needed
- **Empirical:** a statistical method validates a hypothesis; a formal theory may be absent
- **Analytical:** develop a formal theory → compare derived results with observations
- Field-specific: **Design Science Research** (Information Systems), **Design-Based Research** (Education)

### 3.3 Overall research flow

Reasons and outcomes, experience and motivation, and the **literature review** feed into the **research questions** → methods (theoretical or experimental). The literature review also builds the **context / conceptual framework**.

### 3.4 What an answer can be

A **theory, law, model, or method**, or an improvement of one of these.

- **Hypothesis**: an educated guess from a few observations, without experimental evidence
- **Theory**: many observations and a lot of evidence; applies to new relationships; can be modified. Theories are **explanations**.
- **Law**: confirmed over and over; predicts across situations; universal. Laws are **generalisations or patterns**.
- **All scientific knowledge is tentative**, laws included. A scientific "proof" is not a mathematical proof. Tentativeness makes science self-correcting.
- **Model**: a testable idea explaining how part of the world works and predicting similar situations. It is **accurate only under limited situations**. A good model explains as much as possible while staying as simple as possible.
- **Occam's razor** (_Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate_): between models with equivalent predictions, choose the simpler one, the one with the fewest assumptions.
- **Method**: a new or improved process, based on a theory, law or model, or derived from a hypothesis tested experimentally.

### 3.5 Induction, deduction, abduction

| Reasoning | Direction                                          | Guarantees truth?         | Use                                         |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Deduction | Premises → necessary conclusion (B follows from A) | Yes, if premises are true | Checking a law (predict, then test)         |
| Induction | Many instances → generalisation                    | No                        | Proposing laws from accumulated facts       |
| Abduction | Facts → best explanation (B explains A)            | No                        | **Generating hypotheses**; needs creativity |

- Syllogism: all men are mortal; Socrates is a man → Socrates is mortal.
- Black bulls: each black bull **supports** the law but does not prove it. One white bull calls it into question.
- Baconian cycle: evidence → induction → law → deduction → evidence…
- Scientists do **not** all reach the same conclusions from the same evidence. **Creativity is crucial, especially in abduction.**

### 3.6 Experimentation is not simple

Vague claims are not evidence: "the technique is effective", "it works", "results were positive", "I tried it and I like it". You need a way to **measure** effectiveness.

### 3.7 Experimental validation methods (ICT)

**Observational**: data collected as the project develops

| Method             | Description                                    | Weakness                                      | Strength                           |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Project monitoring | Passive collection during a project            | No specific goals                             | Baseline; cheap                    |
| Case study         | In-depth, goal-driven, active; rich insight    | Hard to replicate or generalise statistically | Low cost; can constrain one factor |
| Assertion          | Developers are both experimenters and subjects | Biased                                        | Preliminary test                   |
| Field study        | Monitor several projects; less intrusive       | Treatments differ                             | Inexpensive replication            |

Variations: **ethnography** (the researcher takes part in the group's life) and **survey** (standardised data from many people).

**Historical**: data from completed projects

| Method            | Description                                                       | Weakness                                                          | Strength                |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Literature search | Previously published studies                                      | **Selection bias**; differing treatments                          | Large and cheap         |
| Legacy data       | Quantitative data or artifacts from past projects ("data mining") | Cannot constrain factors                                          | Combines studies; cheap |
| Lessons learned   | Qualitative data from past projects                               | No quantitative data                                              | Shows trends            |
| Static analysis   | Analyse a product's structure                                     | Metric may not reflect the attribute (lines of code ≠ complexity) | Automatable             |

**Controlled**: multiple instances, statistical validity

| Method                | Description                                         | Weakness                             | Strength                                   |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Replicated experiment | Several versions, experimental vs control           | Very expensive; **Hawthorne effect** | Controls all factors; statistical validity |
| Synthetic environment | Lab setting; one factor varied; randomised subjects | Scaling up; real-world interactions  | Controls individual factors; moderate cost |
| Dynamic analysis      | Execute the product (benchmarks)                    | Hard to generalise                   | Automatable                                |
| Simulation            | Execute against a model of the real environment     | Unknown fit to reality               | Safe, fast, cheap                          |

### 3.8 Experiment vocabulary

- **Factor**: the method or tool whose effect on an attribute you study
- **Treatment**: a specific assignment of the factor
- **Subject / experimental unit**: the agent you collect data on
- Goal: enough subjects per treatment for a **statistically significant** comparison
- **Replication**: others can reproduce the findings; use a homogeneous sample or randomise
- **Local control**: **blocking** (a homogeneous population such as students); **factorial design** (every combination of treatments)
  - Note: the slide says 3 factors × 3 values = 9 runs. A _full_ factorial needs 3³ = **27** combinations.

### 3.9 Data

- **Collection instruments**: interviews, observation, questionnaires, documents (including multimedia), log files, physiological data
- **Quantitative analysis**: statistics show relationships are not due to chance. They show trends and probabilities, **not individual cases**.
- **Qualitative analysis**: themes and categories in words or images. Generalise **only to the subjects and settings studied**.
- **Triangulation**: use several instruments to corroborate findings.
  - Types: method, **time**, **space** (countries or cultures), **investigator**
  - Example: users say a site is easy, but logs show heavy use of the help system.

## Lecture 4 — Writing and Presenting Research Work

### 4.1 General principles

- **Clear and concise**; **consistent** notation, format and voice (passive vs first person)
- Research papers vs survey papers. A related-work section is a mini-survey.
- A research paper presents **problem → proposed solution → results**.
- Focus on:
  - **validity**: enough detail to establish it
  - **novelty**: what new, non-obvious knowledge is reported
  - **significance**: what improvement or impact it suggests

### 4.2 Paper structure

- **Title**: concise and specific. Avoid "novel", "performance evaluation", "contribution".
- **Authors**: all three conditions are required:
  1. a significant intellectual contribution
  2. drafting or revising for intellectual content
  3. approval of the final version
- **Abstract**: problem, solution, conclusions; ≤ ~150 words; no general motivation, equations or citations
- **Keywords**: chosen for computerised search
- **Introduction**: motivation and importance → related work (referenced) → brief solution and why it is better → paper roadmap ("The remainder of the paper is organised as follows…")
- **Body**: system model (clear assumptions, figures) → architecture (more generic than your implementation) → realisation
- **Numerical results**: figures or tables; **interpret them** (is it the assumptions or the system?); confidence intervals; enough detail to **reproduce**
- **Conclusions**: summary, **limitations**, **future work**; no new concepts
- **References**: prefer the most accessible version (journal over conference); avoid internal memos, private correspondence, preprints
- **Appendix**: material that is not essential (proofs, protocols, questionnaires)
- **Figures**: referenced in the text; readable on their own; legible
- **Acknowledgments**: funding (grant numbers, required wording) and colleagues

### 4.3 Managing versions (VERSIONS toolkit, 2008)

Track milestone versions: draft → submitted → accepted → publisher's proof → published.

### 4.4 Presentations

- Rehearse and time it (about 1 slide per 1–3 minutes); invite colleagues to ask questions
- Visit the venue beforehand
- Talk to the audience before turning on the projector: the topic, its link to the session, how you got interested
- Keep eye contact; readable visuals; few ideas per slide; explain everything shown; little maths, more figures
- Goal: **why the work matters, its key ideas, how it differs from or improves on existing work**
- Include a **"Contributions"** slide
- Q&A: don't be defensive. Turn hard questions into future work or a coffee-break chat.

### 4.5 Posters, demos, defence

- **Poster**: check the size; title and contacts; eye-catching; graphics over text. Digital arts: usually include a reflective text.
- **Software demo**: test on the actual machine (and battery); arrive early; show the work in a logical order. If it crashes, apologise, move on, and explain what it _should_ do.
- **Thesis defence**: the committee looks for
  1. a deep understanding of the problem and the literature
  2. the contribution
  3. digested results (explain odd ones)
  4. future work
