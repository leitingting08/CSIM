# The Linguistic Mind

## 1. What is language?

- Opening slides: a brain in a jar, and Nobel laureate Donna Strickland ("Not everyone thinks physics is fun but I do", chirped pulse amplification, 2018). Language is how one mind's ideas reach another mind.
- **Formal aspects**: all languages use the same kinds of "elements".
  - All have subjects, verbs and objects.
  - All combine elements.

### 1.1 Core properties of human language

| Property                 | Meaning                                                                                               |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Generative**           | An infinite number of ideas from a finite number of parts                                             |
| **Recursive**            | Can build on itself without limit (structures inside structures)                                      |
| **Displacement**         | Can refer to things that are not present (past, future, absent, imaginary)                            |
| **Modality-independent** | These properties work in any modality: speech is the auditory form, **sign language** the visual form |

### 1.2 Diversity

- Every language represents **a specific solution** to the same problem.
- Language has **different levels** with a **hierarchical organisation** (the "onion" diagram, from the inside out):

  phonetics → phonology → morphology → words → syntax → semantics → pragmatics / discourse

## 2. Levels of language, with cross-linguistic examples

### 2.1 Phonetics: the sounds themselves

- English *th*is (/ð/) vs Spanish da*d*o: similar sounds, produced differently.
- **Tone**: in Chinese, the **pitch** of your voice changes the word's meaning.
- **Clicks** as consonants in **Xhosa**.

### 2.2 Phonology: the sound system's rules

- **Final devoicing**: in German, Dutch, Cypriot Greek, Bulgarian, Korean, Thai and Catalan, voiced obstruents become voiceless at the end of a word: _Madrid → Madrit_.
- **Vowel reduction**: in English, Catalan, Bulgarian and Irish, unstressed positions allow fewer vowels, often **schwa [ə]**.
  - English: the _a_ in _about_, the _e_ in _synthesis_, the _o_ in _harmony_, the _u_ in _medium_
  - Catalan: _mosca → mosquit_
- **Syllable complexity**: Japanese allows (C)V(n); Czech allows up to (C)(C)(C)(C)(C)V(C)(C)(C).

### 2.3 Morphology: how words are built

| Type                         | Description                                                             | Example                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Analytic**                 | Words tend to be **uninflected**                                        | Chinese, Vietnamese; English, Afrikaans                                                                      |
| **Synthetic, agglutinative** | Morphemes are **clearly separated**                                     | Turkish _ev-ler-den_ "from the houses" (_ev_ house + _-ler_ plural + _-den_ from); Korean, Japanese… Klingon |
| **Synthetic, fusional**      | Morphemes are **not easy to separate** from the root or from each other | Most Indo-European languages; Spanish _com-er-ía, com-i-ó, com-er-á_                                         |
| **Polysynthetic**            | Single words equivalent to **whole sentences** in other languages       | Navajo _bééshbeehane'é_ = "a device used for two-way talking with other people" (telephone)                  |

_The slide labels the first synthetic group only "Turkish, Korean, Japanese…" and the third only by its definition; "agglutinative", "fusional" and "polysynthetic" are the standard terms I added._

### 2.4 Syntax: word order

| Order   | Languages                                                                                                                            | Share |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----- |
| **SVO** | English, Spanish, Mandarin                                                                                                           | ~40%  |
| **SOV** | Japanese, Hindi/Urdu, Hungarian, Basque, (Latin)                                                                                     | ~40%  |
| **VSO** | Welsh, Tagalog                                                                                                                       | ~15%  |
| Others  | e.g. **OVS**: only about 11 languages, plus Klingon; Yoda talks this way ("Powerful you have become. The dark side I sense in you.") | ~5%   |

### 2.5 Syntax in processing: relative-clause attachment

- "_Someone shot the servant of the actress **who was on the balcony**._" Who was on the balcony?
  - **Low attachment**: the actress (the nearest noun)
  - **High attachment**: the servant (the first noun)
- The pie charts on the slide show that **English** speakers mostly choose the **actress**, while **Spanish** speakers mostly choose the **servant**.
- So parsing preferences differ between languages: there is no single universal parsing strategy.
- _This is the classic Cuetos & Mitchell (1988) finding; the slide shows the result without the citation._

### 2.6 Manner and path in motion verbs

- **English** packs **manner** into the verb and puts path in a particle: "He **rolled** down the hill"; "The bottle **floated** out of the cave".
- **Spanish** packs **path** into the verb and expresses manner separately, if at all: "**Bajó** la cuesta rodando"; "La botella **salió** flotando de la cueva".
- **Stimulus**: the Canary Row cartoon (Sylvester and Tweety, Merrie Melodies).
- **Gesture follows the language**:
  - English speakers tend to use **one** tumbling diagonal hand motion (manner and path together).
  - Spanish speakers may gesture **first for rolling, then for the downward motion** (separately).

### 2.7 Interfaces

- "Juan vio **a** María por la calle" vs "John saw Mary on the street".
- Levels interact (syntax ↔ semantics ↔ morphology). Spanish marks an animate direct object with **"a"**; English doesn't.
- _The slide gives only the two sentences; this reading is my interpretation._

## 3. The lexicon

### 3.1 What is a word?

- How many words are in "Le miró fijamente y le dijo: dame lo que has escondido en tu bolsillo"? _Dame_ = give + me.
- Catalan _Dona'm-ho_ (give-me-it); French _Est-ce qu'il est venu?_ Word boundaries aren't obvious.
- **How do we recognise words?** How do we know "table" is a word but "tible" isn't? How do we know a picture of a dog is called "dog" in English, not "cat"… or German "Hund"?

### 3.2 The mental lexicon

- Dictionary definitions:
  - "a single unit of language that has meaning and can be spoken or written"
  - "a sound or combination of sounds that has a meaning…"
- A dictionary holds a word's **meaning, pronunciation, syntactic characteristics**, etc. The same goes for the **mental lexicon**.
- **Lexical access** (simplified): the slide shows Strijkers & Costa (2011) and Hickok & Poeppel (2007).
  - Production runs concept/semantics → **lexical** → syllables → phonemes → articulation (spectrotemporal analysis on the perception side).
  - "Cheese" example: semantic → lexical (CHEESE vs MILK, YELLOW, SMELL…) → phonological → motor.

### 3.3 Research paradigms and main effects

- **Lexical decision task**: is this string a real word? (online demos: waltervanheuven.net, PsyToolkit, Hanover)
- **Priming**: semantic (_doctor → nurse_), phonological, etc.
- **Main effects**
  - **Frequency**: common words are recognised faster.
  - **Neighbourhood**: words that differ by one letter or sound. The orthographic neighbourhood of **CAT** includes _cot, mat, hat, rat, cut, cast, cart, car, cap, can_… (Siew, 2018).
  - **Concreteness and imageability**, etc.

### 3.4 Models of spoken word recognition

| Model                                | Key idea                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Cohort** (Marslen-Wilson, 1987)    | The first sounds activate a **cohort** of all words starting that way (_s-_ → sled, stack, stagger…). Candidates are eliminated as more sound arrives until one remains. Stages: access → selection → integration  |
| **TRACE** (McClelland & Elman, 1986) | An interactive connectionist network: **features → phonemes → words**, with activation spreading bottom-up and top-down and competition within each level. _The slide says 1996; the original paper is from 1986._ |

- Through clever manipulation of variables and timing, psycholinguistics has revealed the **dynamics** of the mental lexicon and built **computational (neural-network) models**.
  - Chen & Mirman (2013)
  - Rumelhart & McClelland (1986), parallel distributed processing
  - Backpropagation paper: Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986, _Nature_ 323:533); the slide also shows the 2024 Nobel Prize
- The mental lexicon is a **tangled network of associations** between phonological forms, lexical meanings and more.
  - Open questions: what _are_ "phonological forms" and "lexical meanings"? How are they represented in the brain?

### 3.5 When access fails

- **Tip of the tongue (TOT)**
  - Class demo: the definition of an instrument for measuring angles between celestial bodies in navigation (answer: **sextant**). "Don't say the word aloud!"
  - Record: do you know the word? number of syllables, first sound, words that come to mind.
  - People in a TOT state often know **partial form information** (first sound, number of syllables) without the full word. This shows that **meaning and form are stored separately**.
- **Paraphasias (slips of the tongue)**: the cartoon joke "A Freudian slip is when you say one thing but mean your mother".
- **Anomia**: aphasia in which patients cannot retrieve names (video).

## 4. Language in the brain

### 4.1 Semantic categories (Damasio et al., 1996; replicated 2004)

- 127 adult patients with a single, stable focal lesion. They named 327 pictures of **famous people, animals and tools**. A naming error counted only if the item was properly recognised (a TOT-like failure).
- 97 performed normally and 30 abnormally.

| Lesion site                                                  | Naming impairment            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| **Temporal pole**                                            | Names of **familiar people** |
| **Inferior temporal lobe**                                   | Names of **animals**         |
| **Posterior inferior temporal + anterior lateral occipital** | Names of **tools**           |

→ Different **semantic categories** depend on partly different brain regions.

### 4.2 Embodiment: action words (Pulvermüller, 2005)

- Words for **face, arm and leg** actions (_lick, pick, kick_) activate the corresponding parts of **motor cortex** (somatotopic activation).
- Language meaning is partly **grounded in action systems**. This links to the embodied cognition material in Interaction Models.

### 4.3 The lexical network in the brain (Hagoort, 2020)

- A figure of a production/comprehension network: **lexical concepts → lemma (syntactic word) → phonemes → syllabic motor programmes**, with input and output routes (Levelt-style).

### 4.4 Grammatical class and modality (Caramazza & Hillis, 1991)

- Two aphasic patients:
  - **H.W.** had trouble producing **verbs in speech** but not in writing.
  - **S.J.D.** had the opposite pattern: trouble **writing verbs**, but not saying them.
- Test with **noun/verb homonyms**: _a crack / to crack_, _a watch / to watch_.
  - "There is a \_\_\_ in the window" (noun)
  - "Don't \_\_\_ the nuts in here" (verb)
- The same word form was impaired as a verb but not as a noun, in **one output modality only**.
- → Lexical knowledge is organised by **grammatical class**, with **separate phonological and orthographic output lexicons**.

### 4.5 Sign language

- Sign languages are full natural languages, with their own families (a world map of sign language families).
- **Sign parameters**: handshape, location, movement, orientation (the equivalent of phonology).
- ASL has **morphology** and **grammar** (e.g. time placed first, topic–comment structure).
- This is evidence for **modality independence**.

## 5. Discussion question

> _Would congenitally blind speakers of English and Spanish gesture in the same way when describing the Canary Row video? Why? And what would your answer say about language, and cognition in general?_

**Suggested answer (my addition):**

- Research on this question (e.g. Özçalışkan, Lucero & Goldin-Meadow, 2016, comparing English and Turkish) found that **blind speakers gesture like sighted speakers of their own language**: they conflate or separate manner and path the way their language does.
- They have never _seen_ a gesture, so these gesture patterns are **not learned by visual imitation**. They come from **how the language packages events**.
- So language-specific ways of organising thought ("thinking for speaking", Slobin) shape motor behaviour, and gesture and speech form **one integrated system**.
