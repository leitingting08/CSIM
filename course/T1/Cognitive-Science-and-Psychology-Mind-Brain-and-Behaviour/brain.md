# Prefrontal Lobe: the Executive & Emotional Brain

## 1. Opening

- **Comparative size of the prefrontal cortex (PFC)**: mouse → monkey → human. The PFC is especially large in humans.
- **The flanker (Eriksen) task** (video, plus a child version):
  - Respond to the **central** arrow and ignore the flanking ones.
  - Congruent: → → → → →. Incongruent: ← ← → ← ←.
  - Incongruent trials are slower. This measures **conflict resolution** (the executive network from Posner's model).

## 2. Anatomy of the PFC

Three surfaces: **lateral, medial and orbital**.

| Region                                     | Role                                             |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **Lateral (dorsolateral) PFC**             | **Cognitive aspects of executive functions**     |
| **Medial and orbital (orbitofrontal) PFC** | **Emotional and social regulation of behaviour** |

That split is the lecture's two halves: the **executive** brain and the **emotional / social** brain.

## Part 1 · The executive brain

### 3. Executive functions in action

- **Damage to the PFC does not impair movement or its execution**, but actions become **disorganised** or **inappropriate for current goals**.
- **Perseveration**: repeating an action that has already been done and is no longer relevant.
- **Utilization behaviour**: impulsively using irrelevant objects in the environment.
  - Shallice et al. (1989): a patient was found early in the morning _wearing someone else's shoes_, not responding to simple commands, _putting coins into his mouth and grabbing imaginary objects_; he went round the house _moving furniture, opening cupboards, turning light switches on and off_.
- Both are actions **driven automatically by habits and by objects in the environment**, rather than **controlled behaviour driven by goals**.
- **Key contrast: controlled vs automatic behaviour.**

### 4. Five situations that need executive control (Norman & Shallice, 1986)

1. **Planning or decision making**
2. **Error correction or trouble shooting**
3. **Novel situations**
4. **Dangerous or technically difficult situations**
5. **Overcoming a strong habitual response or resisting temptation**

_In Norman & Shallice's model, routine actions run on automatic schemas; these five situations require the **Supervisory Attentional System (SAS)**, which the PFC implements. The slide lists the five situations; the SAS framing is my addition._

### 5. Tests for each situation

| Situation                      | Test                                                                                                                                                                                | What it shows                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Planning / decision making** | **Tower of London** (variant of the Tower of Hanoi): rearrange balls to match a goal in a minimum number of moves (2, 4, 5 moves on the slide)                                      | The PFC is active during the task in healthy people (imaging), and PFC damage gives poor performance              |
|                                | **Six Elements test**                                                                                                                                                               | An **ecologically valid** test of planning: six sub-tasks must be scheduled within limited time                   |
| **Error correction**           | **Wisconsin Card Sorting Test**: sort cards by colour, shape or number; the rule changes **without warning**                                                                        | Patients keep sorting by the old rule → **perseveration** (no shift in strategy)                                  |
| **Novel situations**           | **Cognitive estimation** ("unknown questions", e.g. how tall is an average woman?); **FAS test** (verbal fluency: name as many words as possible starting with F, A, S in 1 minute) | No routine answer exists, so a strategy must be generated                                                         |
| **Difficult / demanding**      | **Click-counting**; **n-back tasks** (is this item the same as the one n steps back?)                                                                                               | Working-memory load                                                                                               |
| **Overcoming habits**          | **Stroop test**: name the **ink colour** of a colour word (the word "GREEN" printed in red)                                                                                         | Reading is automatic and must be inhibited. Imaging and lesion studies point to the **anterior cingulate cortex** |
|                                | **Simon task**: raise the right hand for blue, left hand for red                                                                                                                    | Responses are slower when the circle appears on the side **opposite** the responding hand (spatial conflict)      |

_The Six Elements test is by Shallice & Burgess (1991). The slides show the Wisconsin, FAS, n-back and Simon tasks without explaining their results; the explanations in the table are standard and added by me._

### 6. Development and the action network

- **The PFC is the last part of the brain to develop.**
  - The slide shows grey-matter maturation from about age 5 to 20 (Gogtay et al., 2004 style): the frontal cortex matures last, in late adolescence and early adulthood.
- **Marshmallow test** (Mischel): can a child wait for two marshmallows instead of eating one now? Self-control develops with the PFC.
- **Action performance involves a network**: **prefrontal cortex** (plans for movement) → premotor cortex and supplementary motor area → primary motor cortex, with parietal (spatial perception), temporal (auditory memory) and occipital (visual perception) inputs.
- Patient videos: planning deficits (three videos).

## Part 2 · The emotional and social brain

### 7. Reading minds

- Thoughts, feelings and intentions are **private and subjective**.
- Yet humans may have evolved skills to **infer** what others think and feel from their behaviour: their facial expression, where they are looking, or by imagining how we would feel in their situation.
- This ability is called **theory of mind**.

### 8. Empathy and simulation theory

- **Simulation theory**: the cognitive resources for **perceiving** an action or emotion are **shared** with those for **producing** it.
- When we see someone hurt or happy, we (literally) feel our own experiences of hurt or happiness, and this lets us empathise.
- Evidence:
  - **Watching somebody being touched** activates our own **somatosensory** system.
  - **Watching a loved one receive a mild electric shock** activates some of the same regions as receiving one ourselves.
- This is the basis of our love of **films and books**, as well as "real" social interaction.
- This links to mirror neurons (Gallese) in the Interaction Models course.

### 9. Autism

- **Leo Kanner (1943)** coined the term "autism". His description of **Donald, aged 5**: stereotyped finger movements, humming the same three-note tune, delight in spinning objects, ignoring people and going straight for objects, shoving away a hand that was in the way.
- **Hans Asperger (1944)** independently described the condition; his name was given to a variant.
- **Defined by**:
  - markedly abnormal development or impairment in **social interaction and communication**
  - a markedly **restricted repertoire of interests and activities**
- It is a **developmental condition noticed before age 3** that persists throughout life. Severity and characteristics can change over time, so it is now regarded as a **spectrum**.
- **Asperger's**: formally defined as **no early delay in language or cognition** (though recent studies suggest the early profile doesn't predict later functioning). Sometimes also used for "high-functioning" autism (normal IQ).
- Figures on the slide:
  - **about 40%** of autistic people have IQs below 70 (the slide uses the outdated term "mental retardation"; today: intellectual disability)
  - **only about 10%** have **savant skills** (drawing from memory, calculation)
- _My addition: since DSM-5 (2013), Asperger's is no longer a separate diagnosis; it is part of **autism spectrum disorder (ASD)**._

### 10. Do autistic children have a theory of mind? The Sally–Anne task

1. Sally puts her ball in her **basket** and goes out for a walk.
2. Anne moves the ball to her **box**.
3. Sally comes back. **Where will Sally look for her ball?**

- Correct answer: **in the basket**. Sally holds a **false belief**, because she didn't see the move.
- _Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith (1985), my addition: most typically developing 4-year-olds and children with Down syndrome answer "basket", while most autistic children answer "box". This suggests a difficulty representing **other people's beliefs** (theory of mind). The slide shows the task and videos without the results._
