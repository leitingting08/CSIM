## 1. Human singularity: what makes us different?

Starting point: the extraordinary **creativity of the human mind** (cave art, drawings, writing, technology).

Candidates for what makes humans unique: **natural language? theory of mind? social structure and culture?** The lecture's answer: **thinking (reasoning)**.

### 1.1 Language and recursion

- **Chomsky & Hauser (2002)** distinguish the faculty of language in the broad sense (FLB) from the **narrow sense (FLN)**. Hypothesis: **FLN contains only recursion**, and recursion is the only uniquely human component of language.
- Example: the cumulative song "Chad Gadya" (_"…that bit the cat, that ate the mouse, that my father bought"_). Clauses are embedded inside clauses.
- **An elementary recursive grammar**
  - Non-terminal symbol: S; terminal symbols: A, B
  - Rules: S → ASA; S → BSB; S → ∅
  - Well-formed sequences are the strings of terminals the rules produce, and **nothing else**: AA, ABBA, BAAB, ABAABA… (mirror-symmetric strings)
  - _The slide shows the derivation steps ASA → AASAA → BAASAAB → ABAASAABA; when S is erased these become the well-formed strings AAAA, BAAAAB, ABAAAABA._
- **But is recursion uniquely human?** Liao, Brecht, Johnston & Nieder (_Science Advances_, 2022) show that **crows** can learn centre-embedded (recursive) sequences, at a level comparable to young children.
- The "Professor Schwartzman" cartoon: a dog-language decoder reveals that barking just means "Hey! Hey!". A joke about how much animal communication actually says.

### 1.2 Language as a "super-power" for thinking

- **Carruthers (2002)**: natural language is the medium of **domain-general thinking**; it integrates the outputs of domain-specific conceptual faculties.
- **Hermer-Vazquez, Spelke & Katsnelson (1999)**: language contributes to the **flexible spatial performance** unique to humans (combining geometry with landmark colour).
- **Xu (1999, 2003)**: learning **names** for things may let infants conjoin two aspects of object representations.

## 3. Two theories of how we reason

|                | **Mental logic** (LOT tradition)                                                                               | **Mental models**                                                                                                                                                                               |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Who            | Braine & O'Brien (1998); Sperber & Wilson (1995); Bonatti (1995, 1998)                                         | Johnson-Laird, Byrne, Schaeken…                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Core idea      | Reasoning = **operations on logical forms**                                                                    | Reasoning is **content-based**: imagine (better, **simulate**) the possibilities                                                                                                                |
| Representation | A **language of thought (LOT)**: a language-like system with **primitives** that respects **compositionality** | A mental model represents "a possibility that has a structure and content that captures what is common to the different ways in which the possibility might occur" (Johnson-Laird et al., 1999) |
| Process        | Comprehension → **select a logical form** → apply **natural deduction rules** → response selection             | Build a model of the premises and read off the conclusion                                                                                                                                       |
| Rules?         | Yes: rules that are psychologically **elementary, primitive and universal**                                    | **No rules, no logical derivations needed**                                                                                                                                                     |

**Example: spatial transitivity**

- Premises: the circle is to the right of the triangle; the triangle is to the right of the square.
- Question: is the circle to the right of the square?
- Mental logic: R(C,T), R(T,S) + transitivity rule (aRb ∧ bRc → aRc) ⇒ R(C,S).
- Mental models: just imagine the line square – triangle – circle and look.

## 4. Early rational reasoning in infants

### 4.1 Hypothesis testing and causal attribution (Gweon & Schulz, _Science_ 2011)

- 16-month-olds press a button on a toy, and **the toy doesn't work** for them.
- Two possible explanations, two possible actions:
  - **"The toy is broken"** → change the **object** (reach for another toy)
  - **"I'm doing it wrong"** → change the **agent** (hand the toy back to the experimenter)
- What infants saw beforehand determined their choice:
  - **Within-object** condition: the same toy sometimes worked and sometimes failed, depending on the person (E1 succeeds, E2 fails…) → the problem is the agent → infants mostly **handed it to the parent or experimenter**.
  - **Between-objects** condition: one toy worked and the other didn't → the problem is the object → infants mostly **reached for the other toy**.
- Conclusion: infants use the **statistical pattern of evidence** to infer the cause of a failure and act on it.

### 4.2 Explaining violations of expectation (Stahl & Feigenson, _Science_ 2015)

- 11-month-olds see an object apparently violate a physical principle:
  - **Solidity**: a ball passes through a wall
  - **Continuity**: an object appears in an impossible place
  - **Support**: a toy rolls off a ledge and stays floating in the air
- Afterwards, infants **learn more about** the object that violated expectations and **explore it more**.
- They explore in a way that **tests the specific hypothesis**:
  - after a **solidity** violation → **more banging** (is it really solid?)
  - after a **support** violation → **more dropping** (does it really fall?)

### 4.3 Goals and the interpretation of actions (Gergely & Csibra, 1999)

- Habituation: a ball **jumps over an obstacle** to reach another ball.
- Test: the obstacle is removed. The ball then either makes the same jump (**old action**) or goes straight (**new action**).
- 9- and 12-month-olds **look longer at the old jumping action**, which is now inefficient, than at the new straight path.
- Conclusion: infants interpret actions **teleologically**. They expect an agent to take the most efficient route to its goal given the constraints.

### 4.4 Imitation vs interpretation

- **Meltzoff & Moore (1977)**: newborns imitate facial gestures such as tongue protrusion and mouth opening (natural imitation).
- **Meltzoff (1988) "head-on-box"**: an adult turns on a light box with their **forehead**; 14-month-olds copy it, even after a delay.
- **Gergely, Bekkering & Király (2002)**: the same demonstration with the adult's **hands free** or **hands occupied** (wrapped in a blanket).
  - Hands free → most infants **use their head** (there must be a reason for it)
  - Hands occupied → most infants **use their hands** (she only used her head because her hands were busy)
  - The effect holds immediately and **after a one-week delay**.
- Conclusion: 14-month-olds do not simply copy novel means. They re-enact them **selectively, depending on whether the situational constraints make the action rational or efficient**. They **imitate intentions**.

### 4.5 Logic vs association (Watson, Gergely, Csányi, Topál, Gácsi & Sárközi, 2001)

- An object is hidden behind one of three screens (A, B, C); the subject searches.
- **Association predicts**: each "not found" is non-reward → **extinction** → searching slows down.
- **Logic predicts** (negated disjunction / elimination of alternatives): not A, not B → **it must be in C** → certainty increases → searching speeds up.
- Result:
  - **children** got **faster** at checking the third screen (logic)
  - **dogs** got **slower** (associative extinction)

### 4.6 Summary: infants as early rational thinkers

- Rationality in **interpreting actions** (Gergely, Csibra)
- Rationality in **hypothesis testing and causal attribution** (Schulz)
- Rationality in **testing alternative hypotheses after violations of expectation** (Feigenson)

## 5. Early irrational reasoning: spectacular failures

- **Inverted-Y tube task (Redshaw & Suddendorf, 2016, 2024)**: a ball is dropped into a tube that splits into two exits. To be sure to catch it, you must cover **both** exits.
  - **2-year-olds and great apes** usually cover only one exit.
  - Success rises with age; most **4-year-olds** cover both from the first trial.
  - They fail to represent **"it might come out left OR right"**, that is, two **possibilities** at once.
- **Mody & Carey (2016)** and **Leahy, Huemer, Steele, Alderete & Carey (PNAS 2022)**: in hide-the-prize tasks with several boxes, children must choose the box that **certainly** holds a prize instead of one that **might**. Young children (≈2.5 years) perform poorly; performance improves around 3–5 years.
- **Leahy & Carey (TICS)**: two kinds of solution
  - **Modal solution**: represent both possibilities explicitly ("it might come out left; it might come out right"). Requires a representation of **possibility**.
  - **Minimal solution**: simulate a single iconic path ("it's going to come out left"), without a concept of possibility. This explains the failures.
- **Rubin's vase (vase or faces)**: perception settles on one reading at a time.
  - **"Perception abhors the unknown. Thought lives with it."**
  - Thinking can hold **"A or B"**; perception cannot.

## 6. Searching for primitives: OR and POSSIBLE without language

### 6.1 Strategy (a "long and painful approach")

- Natural language has many logical and quasi-logical words: AND, OR, NOT, IF, ALL/SOME, NONE, EACH, ANY, MUST, CAN, POSSIBLE, LIKELY, ALWAYS, NEVER, numbers, tense, aspect…
- Pick one primitive, **OR / POSSIBLE**, and study it through the **disjunctive syllogism** (elimination of alternatives):
  - A or B (**representation**: a logical form)
  - not A
  - ∴ B (**reasoning**: an inference)
- The method:
  1. Turn the logical or linguistic inference pattern into a **nonverbal scene**
  2. Find **behavioural and neural markers** of the representation and of the inference
  3. Understand how they **percolate into language**
- Studies: Cesana-Arlotti et al. (_Science_ 2018); Martín-Salguero, Reverberi, Pallier et al. (_Sci. Rep._ 2023); Canudas-Grabolosa et al. (2022); Bohus et al. (_Curr. Biol._ 2023); Martín-Salguero, Gervain, Solari et al. (under review).

### 6.2 The paradigm (Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2018)

- Two objects (e.g. a flower and a snake) go behind an occluder. A cup scoops up **one of them**, but we can't see which → **"x is A or B" (unknown)**.
- Control: the scooped object is visible → **"c is known"**.
- One object then comes out from behind the occluder → **"not A"** → the cup must contain **B (inference)**. Control: no inference needed.
- **Markers**
  - Gaze and pupil changes at the **moment the inference becomes possible**
  - **Surprise** (longer looking) when the cup reveals the logically **impossible** object
- Found in **adults and in 12-month-olds**, and the markers are **stable across development** → logical processes appear constant from infancy.

### 6.3 Neural correlates

- **Adults, fMRI (Martín-Salguero et al., 2023)**
  - **Logical representation** (unknown > known): precentral (BA6, 44), parietal (BA40, 7), **prefrontal (BA10)**
  - **Inference** (inference > no inference): precentral (BA6), parietal (BA7, 40), frontal (BA9, 46/47); occipital regions in the reverse contrast
- **Infants, fNIRS (Martín-Salguero, Gervain, Solari & Bonatti, under review)**: significant oxyhaemoglobin clusters for **inference vs no inference** over frontal, parietal and temporal areas → "POSSIBLE in an infant brain".
- **Background: Tower of London (Shallice, 1982)**
  - A planning task derived from the Tower of Hanoi.
  - Patients with **left anterior (frontal) lesions** were the most impaired as problem complexity increased.
  - This is classic evidence linking the frontal lobes to planning and reasoning.

### 6.4 The possible and the probable

- In lottery-like displays (objects bouncing in a container, one exit), infants are **surprised** when the **improbable** outcome occurs and **anticipate** the probable one.
- Open question: is the representation of **possibility** the **foundation of an intuitive sense of probability**?

## 7. Back to our ancestors: languages of thought (LOT)

- **LOT**: a language-like system of representation with **primitives** that respects **compositionality**.
- Geometric structures are everywhere in human cultures (Lascaux, prehistoric spirals, stone circles).

### 7.1 Geometric sequences (Al Roumi et al., _Neuron_ 2021)

- Participants watch sequences of locations on an **octagon**.
- The proposed LOT has:
  - **Primitives**: rotations (±1, ±2, ±3), symmetries (point, horizontal, vertical, diagonal)
  - **Composition**: repetition, nesting
- Sequence types: repeat, 4 segments, alternate, 4 diagonals, 2 crosses, 2 squares, 2 arcs, 2 rectangles, irregular.
- **Complexity = minimal description length (MDL)**: the length of the shortest program in this language that generates the sequence.
- MDL predicts **encoding time, response time and missed violations**. Shorter programs are easier.

### 7.2 Decoding LOT in the brain (MEG)

- **Decoding at one time point**: train a classifier on one class of stimuli, then test it on **novel** stimuli. Above-chance performance means the brain signal carries that information.
- **Generalisation across time (GAT matrices; King & Dehaene, TICS 2014)**: train at time T (region R), test at T′ (region R′).
  - A narrow **diagonal** means a sequence of different, transient codes.
  - A **square** means a stable, sustained code.
- Al Roumi et al. could decode **ordinal position within a chunk** and the sequence structure, which supports compositional representations.

### 7.3 Geometric shapes: humans vs monkeys (Sablé-Meyer et al., _PNAS_ 2021)

- Task: **spot the odd one out** among quadrilaterals (square, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, kite, trapezoid, irregular…).
- **Geometric regularity effect**: the more regular the shape (square, rectangle), the easier it is to spot a deviant.
  - Found in **French adults, kindergarten children and Himba adults** (no formal geometry schooling)
  - Visual search shows the same effect (slope change with number of items)
- **Baboons**: **no regularity effect**.
- Model comparison:
  - A **symbolic model** (shapes coded by discrete geometric properties such as parallelism, right angles, equal sides) fits **humans**.
  - A **convolutional neural network** fits **monkeys**.
- Interpretation: humans may have a **symbolic geometric language**; other primates rely on perceptual (CNN-like) representations.

### 7.4 Many LOTs?

- LOT-like structure appears in spatial sequences, auditory sequences, geometric shapes, drawings, language…
- **Common** principles:
  - discrete symbols
  - composition (concatenation, iteration, recursion)
  - compression by searching for the **minimal description length**
- Plus **domain-specific** primitives:
  - mathematics: number, size
  - space: location, distance
  - music: pitch, rhythm
  - phonology, syntax, semantics

## 8. Take-home messages

- **Preverbal infants spontaneously draw inferences** (A or B; not A → B).
- **Behavioural markers** reveal inferential activity.
- The **stability of these markers** suggests that logical processes are constant across development.
- Open questions:
  - Is this the foundation of an intuitive sense of probability?
  - Are there primitive languages? Many LOTs?
  - What insights do these findings give into rational thinking?
- **The hard problem**: find out how the **mapping between logic and language** works.
