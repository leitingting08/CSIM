# Memory

---

## 1. Cellular basis (opening figure: Kandel, _Aplysia_)

- A facilitating interneuron releases **serotonin (5-HT)** onto a sensory neuron → **cAMP** → **PKA**.
- **Short-term:** PKA enhances neurotransmitter release onto the motor neuron.
- **Long-term:** PKA (with MAPK) enters the nucleus and phosphorylates **CREB-1**, while CREB-2 (a repressor) is removed. This switches on genes:
  - early genes → ubiquitin hydrolase → **persistent PKA activity**
  - late genes (via C/EBP) → **growth of new synaptic connections**
- Key idea: short-term memory = changes in the strength of existing synapses. Long-term memory = **gene expression + new synapses** (structural change).

## 2. Definition: three interrelated processes

Memory is a hypothetical construct made of three inseparable processes acting on sensory information.

| Process                     | What it is                                                                                                     | Key point                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Registration / encoding** | Input of a stimulus into the system; information is extracted under the conditions in which it was experienced | Necessary but **not sufficient** for storage; may last a fraction of a second; closely tied to **selective attention** |
| **Storage**                 | Information is organised and kept                                                                              | Necessary but **not sufficient** for retrieval                                                                         |
| **Retrieval**               | Accessing stored information                                                                                   | Only stored information can be retrieved, but being stored does **not** guarantee retrieval                            |

## 3. Multi-store model (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968, 1971)

Environmental input → **Sensory stores** → **Short-term store (STS)** ⇄ **Long-term store (LTS)** → response output

| Store            | Content                                                              | Loss       |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Sensory stores   | Visual, auditory, haptic                                             | **Decay**  |
| Short-term store | Control processes: rehearsal, coding, decision, retrieval strategies | Forgetting |
| Long-term store  | "Permanent" memory store                                             | Forgetting |

**In-class demos**

- Letter grids (### / QTY VGM BLC …): brief displays of letter arrays, illustrating how much (and how briefly) a sensory store holds. _(Interpretation: these resemble Sperling-style iconic memory demos; the slides show only the stimuli.)_
- Chess board with circled groups: experts remember positions as meaningful **chunks**.
- Online short-term memory tests (memozor, testable.org): visual and word STM span.

## 4. Working memory (Baddeley, 2002)

**Working memory = short-term memory + control mechanisms.**

- **Central executive**: control and attention; coordinates the subsystems
- **Phonological loop**: acoustic and verbal information
- **Visuospatial sketchpad**: visual and spatial information
- Both subsystems exchange information with sensory input and with long-term memory (verbal store and visual store).

---

## 5. Taxonomy of long-term memory

- **Short-term memory**: sensory memory; short-term / working memory
- **Long-term memory**
  - **Declarative (explicit)**
    - **Episodic**: events; specific personal experiences tied to a time and place
    - **Semantic**: facts; world, object and language knowledge; conceptual priming
  - **Non-declarative (implicit)**
    - **Procedural**: motor and cognitive skills
    - **Perceptual representation system**: perceptual priming
    - **Classical conditioning**: conditioned responses between two stimuli
    - **Non-associative learning**: habituation, sensitisation

## 6. Patient HM (Henry Molaison)

- Epileptic seizures from age 10; operated at **27** (1953).
- **Bilateral medial temporal lobe removal**: hippocampus, amygdala, entorhinal and perirhinal cortices (about a fist-sized portion of brain).
- Result: **profound amnesia**. Decades later he could not recall his last meal, where he lived, or his age (off by up to 43 years).
- But he could still speak and **learn new skills**, so he had not lost "memory" as a whole.
- Conclusion: the **hippocampus is crucial for forming and storing new (declarative) memories**. HM was "frozen in 1953": good memory for pre-surgery events, unable to create new ones.

## 7. Amnesia: causes and types

- **Causes**: neurosurgery (HM), stroke, head injury, certain viruses, long-term alcoholism (**Korsakoff's syndrome**).
- Relevant structures: hippocampus, fornix, mammillary bodies, thalamus, frontal lobe.
- **Anterograde amnesia**: cannot form new memories _after_ the injury (→ present).
- **Retrograde amnesia**: loses memories from _before_ the injury (← past).
- The extent of each varies between patients.

### What is spared and what is impaired?

| System                     | Status         | Evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| -------------------------- | -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Short-term memory          | ✅ Spared      | Normal **digit span** (Baddeley & Warrington, 1970). HM kept a number for 15 min by rehearsing it, then forgot it within 1 min of stopping, with no memory of trying (Milner, 1971).                                                                                                                                    |
| Implicit / non-declarative | ✅ Spared      | **Priming** in word-stem completion (DEFEND → DEF\_\_\_) without recollecting the word. **Procedural learning**: HM's mirror drawing. **Weather prediction task**: amnesics learn the rules but cannot remember which cards they saw. Parkinson's patients show the opposite pattern (poor rule learning, good recall). |
| Semantic                   | ✅ → ❌ Partly | Mostly intact, but most semantic knowledge is learned early in life. **Ribot's law**: older memories are better preserved. Knowledge from a few years before onset is impaired. HM could not learn new vocabulary. Whether other amnesics learn new facts at the same rate or by the same mechanism is debated.         |
| Episodic                   | ❌ Impaired    | The core deficit                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |

- Retrograde gradient (graph): recall of famous events falls from ~65% (1916–1930) to ~0% (1960s onward).

## 8. Amnesia as a consolidation deficit

- **Consolidation**: the process by which moment-to-moment changes in brain activity become **permanent structural changes**.
- It explains anterograde loss. To explain retrograde loss, one must assume older memories were **not yet fully consolidated** at the time of injury (Squire).
- Why does it affect **declarative / episodic** memory specifically? A common view: the hippocampus **binds together information spread across many brain regions** (perceptual, affective, conceptual). This binding needs a **slower mechanism** than learning in cortex.

---

## 9. How information is organised in LTM

- **Schemas**: organised knowledge structures that guide encoding and recall.
- **Encoding specificity (Godden & Baddeley, 1975)**
  - Scuba divers learned word lists on land or underwater, then were tested in the same or the other environment.
  - Recall was better when the **test context matched the learning context** (land–land, water–water).
  - Conclusion: information is best recalled in the environment where it was learned (**context-dependent memory**).

## 10. False memories

**Source misattribution**

- **Cryptomnesia**: remembering an idea but forgetting its source, so believing it is your own (unintentional plagiarism).

**Suggestibility and misinformation (Loftus)**

- Participants watched a red car approaching either a stop sign or a yield sign.
- They were then asked a question that mentioned a **stop** sign.
- Some who had actually seen a yield sign later reported a stop sign.
- Conclusion: misleading information can make people "remember" things that never existed.

**Eyewitness testimony**

- William Jackson served 5 years in prison after two eyewitnesses wrongly identified him. Confident identification ("That's the one!") is not reliable proof.

**Confabulation ("honest lying")**

- Patient H.W., 65, with **frontal lobe damage**, father of four adult children.
- He claimed he had been married for four months and had four children aged 22–32, then explained they were "adopted".
- He produces false memories **without any intent to deceive**.
- The frontal lobes are involved in monitoring and checking memories.

## 11. Memory-impairing drugs

Substances that can cause **anterograde amnesia**:

- GHB (gamma-hydroxybutyrate)
- Rohypnol (flunitrazepam)
- Benzodiazepines
- Ketamine

_(Several are associated with drug-facilitated crimes because victims cannot form memories of events.)_
