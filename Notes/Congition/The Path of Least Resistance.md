# The Path of Least Resistance

_Stop trying to solve the problem._

---

> "Once a new basic structure is in place, the overall thrust of your life — like the power of the river's current — surges to form the results you truly want. And the direct path to those results becomes the path of least resistance."
> — Robert Fritz, _The Path of Least Resistance_

Reading _The Path of Least Resistance_ gave me a name for something I'd been feeling for years but couldn't quite articulate: every meaningful choice I've made in my life was, in its moment, the path of least resistance.

The book is built on three insights:

1. You go through life taking the path of least resistance.
2. The underlying structure of your life determines the path of least resistance.
3. You can change the fundamental underlying structures of your life.

These lead to what Fritz calls the guiding principle: **structure determines behavior**.

Most people assume it works the other way around — that if you change your behavior, your life will restructure itself. It doesn't. Change the structure first, and behavior naturally follows the new path of least resistance. No willpower needed.

This is why willpower-based self-improvement so often fails. You're pushing against a structure that hasn't moved. The moment you relax, the structure pulls you back. Real change isn't wrestling with yourself. It's changing the structure.

---

## "Problem-solving" is a trap

> "When you are in a rocking chair, you will go back and forth. That is the nature of the structure. This is not a problem to solve, but a matter to understand." — Robert Fritz

When you think in terms of problems, you end up trapped in what Fritz calls the **reactive-responsive orientation** — you either react against your circumstances or respond to them. Either way, the circumstances are in charge. And the solving itself is deceptive: problems don't actually get resolved, they get displaced by bigger ones. Each fix feels like progress, but all you've done is reshuffle the same structure.

The way out isn't a better solution. It's a different starting point. When you begin with "what do I want to create" instead of "what's wrong," everything shifts. You stop fighting or adapting. You move toward something, and the old problems either dissolve on their own or quietly stop mattering.

---

## Compensating strategies

Inside an ill-fitting structure, people develop coping mechanisms to make life bearable. Fritz calls these **compensating strategies**. The revenge weekends after a draining work week. The people-pleasing or emotional withdrawal inside an unequal relationship.

> "Much of the advice people give each other will not work, because it is designed to change compensating strategies without any notion of the underlying structures that are causing them." — Robert Fritz

When someone tells you to stop staying up all night, stop people-pleasing, "learn to relax" — they're treating the surface. You might spend enormous effort trying to fix these behaviors, but as long as the underlying structure stays the same, the symptoms will return in new forms.

Changing structures looks more expensive than patching behaviors. It isn't. Once the structure shifts, the compensating strategies simply disappear. They no longer have a reason to exist.

---

## Structural tension

Structural tension has two components:

1. A vision of the result you want to create.
2. A clear understanding of your current reality.

When both anchors are sharp, a tension naturally forms between them. This tension is not an unpleasant feeling to be resolved. It's the energy source of creation itself. Like a drawn rubber band, it wants to move toward resolution — and that pull is what carries you forward.

Most people's instinct, when they feel this tension, is to discharge it as fast as possible. You react by trying to change reality, or you respond by lowering the vision — either way, you're trying to make the tension go away.

The result: you oscillate. You push for a while, get tired, make peace with things, get restless, push again. You look busy. You haven't moved.

The creative approach is the opposite: you don't dissolve the tension. You preserve it and use it. You hold both ends in view — where you are, where you want to go — and let the pull between them do the work. No willpower required. What you need is an honest understanding of where you are, and the willingness to make what Fritz calls fundamental, primary, and secondary choices consistent with your vision.

One point that gets overlooked: **a clear understanding of current reality is as important as vision**. People assume vision is the whole game — that if you just want it hard enough, the rest follows. But if your read on the present is blurry, idealized, or evasive, the tension never forms. You need two sharp anchors, not one.

---

## The cycle of creation

Once vision and current reality are both in view, structural tension starts doing its work. But creation isn't a single event. It's cyclical.

Fritz describes three phases, each generating a different kind of energy that carries you into the next:

**Germination** is where everything begins. You've glimpsed the vision, but it's still fragile, diffuse, vulnerable to the noise of the present. The energy here is a mixture of excitement and unease. Protect the seed before it has roots — don't let the first wave of reality talk it out of existing.

**Assimilation** is the phase where vision becomes ordinary. The initial thrill fades. The work turns repetitive, mundane, sometimes boring. This is where most people quit, because there's no novelty to fuel them and no finished product to reward them. But assimilation is where the vision becomes part of you. The energy is quieter, steadier, lower. Sit with that lowness. Let it in.

**Completion** is bringing the thing out into the world. What this phase asks for isn't the excitement of beginnings or the patience of middles. It's the willingness to end. People often stall here, because ending means judgment — it means accepting that what you made isn't perfect. But only by finishing can you release the energy that moves you into the next germination.

What's worth noticing: these three energies aren't additive. They cycle. The end of one completion is already the beginning of the next germination. Creation isn't a thing you do once. It's a way of living in which you're always somewhere in a cycle — something germinating, something assimilating, something completing.

---

## Soil and seeds

From my own experience, the one thing I've actually done — the only thing — is keep putting myself into different soils.

Early on, I didn't choose my soil — I was placed in it. You don't choose your early environment. Later, I learned to actively place myself in soils where I thought the things I wanted might grow.

What surprised me: seeds I had planted long ago, that had never sprouted anywhere I'd been, suddenly came up in new soil. Nothing had changed about the seed. The soil had been wrong. So I stopped trying to fix soils. I started changing them. And all kinds of shoots I hadn't expected began appearing on their own.

Of course it isn't only soil. There's sunlight, rain, timing — I give these room too. I try to sense whether an environment fits in the moment, rather than labeling it in advance. The more I do this, the better I get at sensing, and the clearer the vision becomes. When you know what you want to grow into, structural tension carries you along the path of least resistance.

At some point I realized: what I had been calling "soil" is what Fritz calls _structure_. Same thing, two vocabularies. When an old structure stops working and all that's left is structural conflict, the move isn't to sit in it and grieve. The move is to create a new structure.

---

## Cognition before action

What I've come to feel most strongly through all of this: when you're deeply dissatisfied with your current reality, the first move isn't to solve the problem. It's to change your cognition first. **Cognitive reconstruction, to me, is the germination phase of the creative cycle — planting seeds while the vision is still unclear.**

Do whatever changes your cognition. Read things you wouldn't normally read. Meet people unlike the ones you know. Go to places that make you feel unfamiliar to yourself. Cognition reshapes language, language reshapes behavior, behavior forms new structures — and the new structure quietly starts carrying you where you actually want to go.

I used to be stuck on "what should I do." Now my questions are "what do I want" and "where am I right now." The old question paralyzes. The new ones start you moving, almost without effort.

This is just structural tension, applied at the scale of a day. "What should I do" is problem-oriented — it assumes something has to be solved, and it drops you into the reactive-responsive binary. "What do I want" and "where am I right now" are creation-oriented. They set up the two anchors, and the tension between them does the pulling.

---

The path of least resistance isn't a skill to master. It's what everyone, everywhere, is already doing. We all move along whichever path costs the least energy in whatever structure we currently inhabit. That's not a feat. It's how energy moves.

The real difference is in what structure you're in — and how you use its structural tension.

Over these past few years, I've stopped trying to discharge the tension and started using it. For me, changing soils means changing the structure so the tension can keep doing its work — instead of being neutralized by reaction or response, it quietly carries me where I actually want to go.

I'm standing in front of new soil again. I don't know what will grow this time. But I've gotten used to not knowing.
