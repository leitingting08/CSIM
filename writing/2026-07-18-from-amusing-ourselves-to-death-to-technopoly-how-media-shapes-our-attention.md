> _There is no definitive history of anything; there are only histories, human interventions which do not give us the answer, but give us only those answers called forth by the questions that have been asked. — Neil Postman,_ Technopoly

Lately I’ve been paying attention to attention itself.

From a cybernetic perspective, attention itself is a kind of language. Wittgenstein said language is not only the vehicle of thought but its engine.

If information means recognizing structure within uncertainty, then attention filters certain patterns out of an overwhelming field of possibilities, making them easier to process and understand.

I’ve been reading Neil Postman’s trilogy —  _Amusing Ourselves to Death_ , _The Disappearance of Childhood_ , and _Technopoly_ — which references McLuhan’s line: the medium is the message. Media may matter more than we think. They don’t just carry content; they shape how we perceive and think in the first place.

If attention is a filter, the next question follows naturally: what rules govern this filtering, and who decides them?

### The Disappearance of Childhood and _Amusing Ourselves to Death_

Taken together, Postman’s three books read like a progression. _The Disappearance of Childhood_ and _Amusing Ourselves to Death_ deal with specific shifts in media; _Technopoly_ keeps pressing further: beneath these shifts, what exactly is changing.

Literacy was once a threshold that required sustained attention to cross — and that threshold itself drew the line between “child” and “adult.” Television lowered the barrier to entry into public information. Images require none of the long, systematic training that literacy demands; most people can understand them directly. As the way we access information changed, the boundary that literacy had built between childhood and adulthood began to loosen.

What’s worth noting is that this collapse carries a certain self-undoing irony. It was literate civilization’s own knowledge and technology that eventually produced television, a new medium — and once that new medium arrived, it began eroding the very centrality of the written word it had come from.

Television did something further still. Postman called it a “meta-medium”: it doesn’t just influence what we come to know, but also how we come to know it. Politics, education, religion: all were eventually re-encoded by television’s epistemology into the shape of entertainment. What survives this filtering tends to be whatever can be made entertaining. Information gained through reading can connect to knowledge we already hold; information delivered by television tends to arrive as isolated, disconnected fragments. The real problem may not be the sheer volume of information, but whether that information can form meaningful connections and structure. Continuous context helps us build these connections; information sliced into fragments can pile up endlessly while understanding fails to keep pace.

These two books still dismantle something concrete — a specific threshold, a specific medium. _Amusing Ourselves to Death_ is about television. Today, phones and streaming media have let that same logic seep out of public broadcasting and into daily life, competing for our attention without pause. _Technopoly_ , by contrast, asks a more fundamental question: what standards do we use to judge the world in the first place?

### Technopoly

Postman argued that every tool carries an embedded ideological bias — a tendency to construct the world one way rather than another, to amplify a particular sense, skill, or capacity at the expense of others. No tool is ever a neutral container; it’s just that this bias is rarely brought up for discussion.

Technopoly is about how that bias slowly becomes common sense that no longer needs explaining. In earlier eras, technology was simply one value system among many. Under technopoly, efficiency, speed, and expansion become self-evident goods in their own right — and every other value system has to justify itself according to technological standards. Anything that hasn’t been digitized, quantified, or accelerated starts to look suspect, needing to defend its own right to exist.

The book describes a mindset it calls “scientism”: once the old sacred narratives lost their hold, people turned to science to find a new, ultimate moral authority — as if precise calculation could substitute for judgment, as if more information automatically meant deeper understanding. Postman’s reminder is that these are never the same thing: information is a specific account of the world, while understanding requires the work of filtering and integration. Conflating the two is one of technopoly’s deepest illusions.

There’s another detail worth sitting with: a word — even the most important word — repeated often enough, said everywhere enough, quickly exhausts its symbolic value and collapses into meaningless noise. The world of technopoly doesn’t lack information. What it lacks is the context that lets information take root. Information gets produced faster and faster; symbols get worn thin faster and faster.

### What Resistance Looks Like

Near the end of _Technopoly_ , Postman sketches a portrait of what he calls “the resistance fighter against technopoly.”

They keep a certain distance from anything that presents itself as objective, precise, scientific. A number’s precision alone doesn’t convince them it’s close to truth; a method labeled “scientific” doesn’t automatically override their own judgment.

They don’t treat efficiency as everything, and they don’t equate more information with deeper understanding. Technology can certainly help us calculate, measure, accelerate — but it can’t answer for us: what is worth pursuing?

So they continue to take seriously the things that resist easy quantification: common sense, tradition, family, loyalty, honor, religion, and the kinds of human relationships that do not need to be measured in order to matter.

They admire technical ingenuity and invention, but don’t mistake technology for the highest form of human achievement.

They don’t reject technology outright — they simply refuse to treat it as the natural order of things. They understand that every technology emerges from a specific context, carrying its own biases and interests.

So they keep just enough distance from technology. Only then can they avoid mistaking the world in front of them for the only possible one.

### Brave New World

What Orwell feared was control imposed from outside — truth hidden by power, books banned, thought under surveillance. What Huxley feared was something else entirely — no one needing to ban books, because no one wants to read them anymore; no one needing to suppress the truth, because truth drowns in an ocean of endless, unrelated information.

Postman believed the world we live in leans closer to Huxley’s vision. Media doesn’t need to force us to believe anything — it only needs to determine, in advance, what shape information takes to reach us. What follows is not forced acceptance, but acceptance we complete ourselves.

When Postman wrote these books, his main target was still television.**** Since then, phones and streaming platforms have amplified the same media logic. Television at least had fixed time slots and channels; at some point in the twenty-four-hour day, the television was off. The smartphone has no such boundary. Notifications can arrive at any hour, and the threshold for accessing information has fallen almost to zero.

Information overload, in a sense, is what happens when the filtering process becomes invisible. Signal and noise arrive together, at roughly the same speed, and sheer quantity becomes the least important part of the problem.

What we may need to filter is not only information itself, but the very way information arrives at us.
