_The mind doesn’t just experience the world. It can also turn back and observe itself experiencing it._

Lately I’ve caught myself observing all the time — even observing my own observing. And along with that, there’s this nagging sense that I might be over-observing. I’m living my life, but there always seems to be another version of me standing off to the side, watching.

I wrote about a moment like this in my year-end review last year. One morning I was walking under the trees at Chiang Mai University, a song from the film (Journey to the West, Kong Dashan’s 2021 film — not the classical novel) playing in my headphones. I’d finished the film not long before, and it was still vivid in my mind. Walking the shaded campus path in the morning light, as the music swelled, the tears came before I could stop them. First I noticed I was crying. Then, almost immediately, that automatic analyzing brain of mine started up again, working out why I was crying at this particular moment.

My attention seems to tilt, by default, toward explaining the world. But it costs something: the world sometimes turns into an object waiting to be explained, instead of something I’m actually living through. I’ve come to think that part of why I default to the observer’s seat is the quiet safety it offers. It lets me get close to the world without fully exposing myself to it.

As a psychological setup, the observer’s position is dangerously appealing. It gives me three things:

**Distance:** No matter how deeply I enter, I always keep a way back, so I never feel completely trapped.

**Control:** If I think through the risks beforehand, I am less likely to be caught off guard.

**An exit:** The moment something feels wrong, I can step away.

But here’s the part I find interesting — the observer isn’t only a defense. Yes, it holds me a little apart from whatever’s happening. But that same distance is what gives it a strange gift for recording. Often, when I’m genuinely absorbed in a moment, the observer doesn’t switch off. It just stops analyzing and starts recording instead. And what it records isn’t only the event — it’s the whole field the event happened in: the light that particular morning, a smell in the air, the song in my headphones, how I was feeling at the time. So when I look back, what stays with me is rarely an isolated event. It’s a whole scene.

Psychology has a concept that helps explain this: context-dependent memory. Memory doesn’t file an event away the way a hard drive saves a document. More often it encodes the place, the smell, the sound, the state of your body, the emotion — all bound together. It’s why, years later, a song can drop you straight back into a specific moment: you’re not opening a file, you’re stepping back into an entire environment. The reason that morning in Chiang Mai — the shade, the song, the tears — is still so sharp is precisely that it got encoded and filed like that, on the spot. And those saved moments end up shaping me more than I realize. A lot of the choices I make, when I follow them back to their source, lead to some moment the observer had quietly kept.

#### Metacognition: how we became our own observers

There’s another idea in psychology called metacognition. Simply put: not just thinking, but being aware that you’re thinking. Ordinary thought is “I like this.” Metacognition is “I notice that I like this, and I want to know why.” It’s like installing a mirror inside your own mind — one that lets you see your own thoughts, feelings, and behavior. It’s what lets us catch our own patterns, correct our own mistakes, step out of our automatic reactions.

In _Thinking, Fast and Slow_ , Daniel Kahneman lays out the mind’s two systems: System 1, fast, automatic, running on intuition; System 2, slow, effortful, doing the analyzing and reasoning and reflecting. In a way, metacognition is System 2 keeping an eye on System 1 — the thing that keeps us from being wholly run by our first reaction, that lets us stop and ask: wait, why did I just think that?

But this is exactly where the trouble starts. The very ability that gives us freedom can also be what pulls us out of the present. Because observing, by its nature, means standing at a distance. Watch a flower, and there’s a gap between you and the flower. Watch your own feeling, and a small gap opens between you and your own experience. It’s an odd human bind: we need enough distance to see ourselves clearly — and too much of it, and we can’t really be inside anything anymore.

#### Coming back into the room

Life can’t be fully modeled. Information theory says the world always carries noise. Cybernetics says a system has to keep correcting itself against feedback. A human life is no different. You can’t solve it all in advance with one perfect model, because a person isn’t a closed system, a relationship isn’t an equation you can finish solving, and a life isn’t a program waiting to be optimized.

So maybe the real point of metacognition isn’t to keep us standing outside life for good. Maybe it’s to let us step back when we need to, find our bearings, and then step back into the experience itself once we no longer need the distance.

The observer isn’t the enemy. It has helped me understand the world; it’s given me the capacity to reflect, to make things, to change. But a life can’t happen entirely inside a mirror. Understanding yourself is meant to help you become more freely yourself — not to turn yourself into your own research subject. That self who’s always off to the side, watching, maybe doesn’t need to vanish. It just needs to know that sometimes it can put the notebook down and come back into the room.
