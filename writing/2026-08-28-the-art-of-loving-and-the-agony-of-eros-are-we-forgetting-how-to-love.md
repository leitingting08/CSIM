_Maybe what we lack today isn’t the capacity to love, but the capacity to let another person remain beyond our understanding._

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*FNWAnRX2LQ0aR1sN759sqA.png)

> _By now everyone has forgotten a startling fact: that at the very beginning of philosophy and theory, reason and eros were intimately bound together. Without the force of eros, reason too grows powerless._

> _— Byung-Chul Han,_ The Agony of Eros

A few days ago, a friend and I were talking about something she thought younger people were missing. She called it “a blind faith in life.”

I had just finished reading Erich Fromm’s _The Art of Loving_ and Byung-Chul Han’s _The Agony of Eros_. As I was trying to make sense of what Han means by “the disappearance of the Other,” her words suddenly came back to me.

She went on to say that she has grown tired of reading things online, tired of concepts and of so-called “reason.” There seem to be too many ready-made explanations out there: an emotion can be categorized, a behavior analyzed, a relationship defined. We’ve become very good at understanding life through concepts, so good that we often reach for the explanation first, and only then look at what is actually happening.

But I love the way she and I talk things through. I catch myself instinctively trying to understand what she says in terms of my own concepts, translating a vague feeling into language I’m familiar with. And she keeps pushing the concept away: “That’s not it.” Then she finds her own words for it again. So we keep moving closer, and keep correcting each other’s understanding.

Looking back, what strikes me isn’t whether we ever arrived at a precise answer, but that throughout the process, some part of what we were trying to say was never exhausted by any concept. She never fully entered my understanding, and I never fully entered hers. Each of us spoke from our own experience, language, and view of the world, while continually encountering, in the other, something we couldn’t immediately absorb.

Maybe that is exactly where the Other still exists. A true Other shouldn’t be someone I can quickly understand, explain, and file away. There should always remain something about them that cannot be fully absorbed into my understanding.

#### The Capacity to Love

> Love is an active power in the human being — a power that breaks through the walls between one person and another, that unites us with others; love lets us overcome the sense of isolation and separation, and yet lets us remain ourselves, keeping the integrity of who we are.

Fromm opens _The Art of Loving_ with a question: **Is love an art?** If it is, then it requires knowledge and effort.

Most people, Fromm argues, believe that love is a pleasant feeling, a matter of chance, something one “falls into” if one is lucky. People hunger for love, and yet almost no one thinks there is anything about it that has to be learned. This attitude, he says, rests on several unexamined assumptions.

The first is that most people see the problem of love as being loved, rather than loving. So the question becomes: How can I be loved? How can I become lovable? And in our culture, being “lovable” usually means little more than being both popular and sexually attractive.

The second assumption is that love is a problem of the object, rather than of a capacity. People believe that once they meet the “right person,” love will simply happen. If love hasn’t come yet, it must be because the right person hasn’t appeared.

The third lies in confusing the initial experience of “falling” in love with the lasting state of “being” in love. The first rush of attraction, infatuation, and novelty easily produces the feeling that “this is it.” Two people are drawn together and experience an intense sense of union. That state of intoxication is often taken as proof of the intensity of love. But Fromm thinks this intensity may only prove how lonely the two people were beforehand.

Falling in love, after all, may require very little skill. What is truly difficult is what comes after the initial passion fades, when the other person reappears as an independent human being: with their own desires, boundaries, flaws, and parts that cannot be fully understood. At that point, do we still have the capacity to keep loving?

> _There is hardly any activity, any enterprise, that is begun with such tremendous hopes and expectations, and yet fails so regularly, as love._

Fromm doesn’t conclude from this that love is impossible. On the contrary, he insists that love is an art. And if it is an art, it can be learned.

His argument begins from an existential premise: human beings are uniquely aware of their separation from the world. To be aware of separation is to be alone; and aloneness produces anxiety. So we keep looking for ways to dissolve this sense of separateness.

Orgiastic states can suspend the distance for a while. Conformity lets us hide inside “being like everyone else.” Creative work reconnects us with what we are making. But none of these truly resolves separation.

Love is another kind of answer: **not the erasure of boundaries, but a union in which each person remains whole.**

“Remaining whole” matters. It is what separates love from swallowing or possessing. To show how such love is possible, Fromm names four elements: **care, responsibility, respect, and knowledge.**

“Respect” is worth pausing on. The Latin root _respicere_ carries the sense of looking. To respect a person is to see them as they are, and to want them to grow in their own way, rather than becoming what I need them to be.

And real respect presupposes independence. Only when I no longer need to dominate another person in order to confirm myself can I truly see them.

If love is an art, it takes practice. Fromm talks about discipline, concentration, and patience, and points to an obvious contradiction: we are willing to spend years learning a musical instrument, yet we assume love needs no teacher.But Fromm also saw a larger problem.In a “personality market,” people learn to price themselves, to sell themselves as commodities. And so the union of two people easily becomes a transaction that looks like a fair deal: each supplies value, each meets the other’s needs, and together they hold off the outside world.

Love gets downgraded into a refuge, even a kind of “teamwork.” Fromm calls this _égoïsme à deux_ : two lonely people forming an alliance without ever really moving toward each other.Yet Fromm remains an optimist.Because if love is an art, then it isn’t fate. It is a capacity that can be learned, practiced, and gradually mastered.

#### The Agony of Eros

When Fromm wrote _The Art of Loving_ , he was already looking at the commodification of intimacy: people treating themselves as commodities, love turning into an exchange.Han pushes the problem one step further. The issue is no longer only that we treat people as commodities, but that **the Other itself is becoming harder to encounter.**

We have more ways than ever of knowing other people, and more knowledge about people in general. Personality tests, psychology, attachment theory, social media — all of it helps us understand ourselves, and helps us judge others.

**We’re getting better and better at figuring people out. But not necessarily better at seeing them.**

The “eros” Han is talking about isn’t only romantic love or sexual desire in the ordinary sense. For him, eros is closer to a force that carries me beyond myself.

**Eros interrupts the closed circle of the self.** I am able to love not just because I like someone, but because I am moved by an existence different from my own.

The Other is not my mirror, nor my projection. They can disturb my order, expose me to something I hadn’t anticipated, even force me to leave, for a moment, that familiar, controllable, self-consistent self.

Eros, then, carries within it something uncontrollable. And modern society works harder and harder to eliminate exactly that. We want everything to be understandable, explainable, predictable, even selectable in advance.

We don’t only analyze a person; we have begun to screen one: Are the values aligned? Is the personality a match? Is the attachment style compatible? Are the interests shared? It all sounds more rational than before. But the problem is not understanding itself. **The problem is the fantasy that understanding can leave nothing unknown.**

If I’ve already decided that someone is “right for me” before I truly encounter them, have I really encountered them at all?

The Other is not simply the person beside me. The Other is an existence genuinely different from me. They have their own desires, experiences, and world. I cannot fully absorb them into my understanding, nor can I predict them through a set of standard answers. And love is possible precisely because the Other is not me.

If what I am facing is only an object that fits my preferences, meets my needs, and can be understood and predicted by me, then what I get may be intimacy, companionship, even pleasure — but not love in Fromm’s sense. Because love needs more than closeness.

**It needs difference. It needs distance. It needs an Other who cannot be completely possessed by me.**

This brings me back to the way my friend and I talk. We keep correcting each other’s understanding. Precisely because we never completely understand each other, the Other always retains something of their own.

Maybe intimacy isn’t finally understanding a person through and through. Maybe it is continuing to move closer while still allowing them to keep the part that cannot be understood by me.

In other words, **the difficulty of love begins exactly where the fantasy of complete union ends.**

When we become increasingly accustomed to analyzing, categorizing, screening, and understanding everything, are we actually getting closer to the Other — or are we simply turning the Other into something that can be understood by us? Fromm asks: **Do I have the capacity to love?** Han asks something further: **If the genuine Other is disappearing, whom are we still able to love?** And maybe this is where I finally understood what my friend meant by “a blind faith in life.”

**Maybe that blind faith is simply the willingness to commit to life without fully understanding it, without having all the answers.**

It is true of loving a person, and perhaps of living itself. We can’t fully understand someone before deciding whether to love them; nor can we explain life clearly before deciding whether to commit ourselves to it.

Maybe what we lack today isn’t the capacity to love, but the capacity to let a person remain beyond our understanding — and to let ourselves be moved by an Other we can never fully understand.

Perhaps a person remains a true Other precisely because there will always be something about them I cannot fully understand.

**And love, perhaps, begins right there.**
