_Interest explores. Friction decides what stays._

Lately, I’ve become increasingly aware of how little energy I have.

Small chores seem to swallow most of it. When I start something new, my first thought is usually, “Sure, sounds good, why not?” I begin the moment I get interested. But after a while, sometimes before the thing is even finished, I’m already tired, and a voice says: maybe just let it go.

In Chinese, there’s a phrase for this: _三分钟热度_ , literally “three-minute heat.” A burst of enthusiasm that fades almost as quickly as it arrives.

I have a lot of it. I see someone play guitar well, and I want to learn. I see someone draw beautifully, and I want to learn. I see beautiful calligraphy, and I want to learn. I buy the stationery, the tools, the gear, and the heat lasts less than three minutes. Everything ends up gathering dust, and I’ve mastered none of it.

I’ve always been easy on others and easy on myself. I’d comfort myself with lines like “a person’s energy is limited” and “giving up is a kind of virtue.” I also used to put it down to a short attention span. But lately, I’ve started to rethink that.

**Why can a person become interested in so many things so quickly, yet form lasting, low-friction habits around only a few?**

### Interest Explores, Behavior Exploits

In cognitive science and reinforcement learning, there’s a classic problem called the _exploration–exploitation trade-off_. **Exploration** means trying new possibilities to discover something valuable. **Exploitation** means continuing to invest in what you already know works, using experience to get more reliable returns.

I see guitar, drawing, calligraphy, and I feel interested. Then I try them. That’s exploration.A lot of interests are probably meant to be filtered out along the way. The more interesting question is: **what makes an interest cross the line from exploration into exploitation?**

Educational psychology has a related distinction. An interest sparked by a video, a person, or an impressive performance can be described as **situational interest**. It arrives quickly and can disappear just as quickly. **Individual interest** , by contrast, is the kind that makes you want to return to something on your own.

Getting from the first to the second takes more than enthusiasm. It takes enough energy to keep coming back.

### Energy Is the Cost of a Behavior

This reminds me of _The Power of Full Engagement_. Its central claim is simple: **what needs managing isn’t time, but energy.**

Energy isn’t only physical. The book describes four dimensions: **physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual**. More importantly, energy is something we spend and renew. Instead of demanding constant high performance, we can build a rhythm of _engage → recover → engage again_.

This gives me a different way to think about behavior. Energy is how much I have available. Friction is what a behavior costs me each time I do it. And that cost isn’t just time. It includes attention, working memory, decision-making, emotional regulation, and even the mental effort required to get started.

So sometimes “forget it” may not mean “I don’t want this anymore.” It may simply mean: **the cost of continuing has become higher than what I’m willing to pay right now.**

We often think of persistence as willpower: you don’t feel like doing something anymore, but you force yourself to continue. But if a behavior requires willpower every single time you start it, perhaps the problem isn’t a lack of discipline. Perhaps the friction is simply too high.
