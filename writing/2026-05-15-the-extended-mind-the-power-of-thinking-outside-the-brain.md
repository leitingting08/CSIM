> The mind is something more like the nest-building bird I spotted on my walk, plucking a bit of string here, a twig there, constructing a whole out of available parts.

A few days ago I was harvesting rapeseed in the fields back home.

I started with an audiobook. Before long, nothing was getting in. I switched to music — that helped, for a while. Then the work got harder, and even the music became too much. I turned off my phone and just worked.

Apparently listening to books and music takes effort too.

I’ve been reading Annie Murphy Paul’s _The Extended Mind_ , and it might explain why.

The book opens with a counterintuitive question: does thinking actually happen only inside the brain? In 1998, philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers published a paper asking: “Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin?” Their answer challenged an assumption we rarely question: the skull and skin are not as sacred as we think. Elements of the external world can extend our minds, letting us think in ways that exceed what the brain alone can manage.

Paul takes this abstract idea and grounds it in three concrete things: our bodies, the spaces we inhabit, and the people around us.

### Embodied Cognition

> To make good decisions, listen carefully to what your body is telling you.

When my thoughts get too tangled, I copy calligraphy.

Static meditation doesn’t work well for me — my mind just wanders. But copying characters, which demands hand-eye coordination, gradually quiets things down. When I have no ideas at all, I go for a walk. Thoughts tend to surface on their own.

The book has a name for what’s happening: _interoception_ — the perception of the body’s internal states: heartbeat, breath, the sensations in the gut. The body processes information at a speed and complexity that often exceeds conscious thought. People with stronger interoceptive awareness tend to have more reliable intuitions.

So how do we use this capacity?

Step one: listen. Step two: name what you feel. Research shows that simply labeling an interoceptive sensation has measurable effects on the nervous system — it can quickly reduce the stress response and lower activity in the amygdala, the brain structure involved in processing fear and strong emotions. Overanalyzing feelings, by contrast, tends to increase amygdala activity. Name it first. Don’t rush to explain.

Paul offers a practical tool: an _interoceptive diary_. Three parts — first, describe the problem and the possible choices; second, imagine each option one by one and record what you feel in your body; third, note the final decision and any sensations that accompanied it. Done consistently, the body’s signals become readable.

The body also shapes thinking through movement itself. Moderate exercise — a walk — produces a level of physiological arousal that sharpens the mind. High-intensity exercise — running — temporarily reduces prefrontal activity, letting thoughts and feelings flow more freely. Sometimes that’s when the unexpected ideas arrive. Interoceptive awareness also tells us when to push and when to stop.

One last thing: gestures are thinking too. Research shows that gesturing helps us grasp abstract concepts, reduces cognitive load, and improves memory. As one researcher put it, riffing on Descartes: _I move, therefore I remember._

### Situated Cognition

 _Where do we like to be?_

> Humans evolved strong preferences for certain natural environments — open grassland, scattered trees, water within reach, and just enough mystery around the corner to promise discovery. This isn’t aesthetic taste. It’s written into our biology.

Screens work in the opposite direction. Devices are designed to capture attention and hold it. They conflict with the mind-wandering and default mode network activation that underlies creative thought. Rachel and Stephen Kaplan called this Attention Restoration Theory: natural environments offer “soft fascination” — gentle, involuntary attention that lets the directed kind recover. Nature is one of the few things that genuinely pulls us away from screens.

Nature can also bring on a rarer state: awe. Studies show that people experiencing awe have reduced activity in the brain regions associated with self-perception and spatial orientation — the boundary between self and other softens, and people feel part of something larger. Behaviorally, awe makes people more prosocial, more generous.

Back indoors, built spaces shape thinking too. When the brain is occupied, it defaults to shortcuts — familiar assumptions, well-worn approaches. The design of a space — ceiling height, quality of light, a plant on the desk, the arrangement of walls — quietly determines what kind of thinking is possible.

Zoom in further: the objects we keep around us. Our sense of self is not stable. It’s fluid, dependent on external structure. Anyone who has traveled somewhere entirely unfamiliar knows the feeling — the environment doesn’t know us, and we briefly lose our footing. In daily life, the objects near us help maintain a stable sense of who we are. As Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi wrote, we attend to certain objects because “they tell us things about ourselves that we need to hear in order to keep our selves from falling apart.”

Objects help us think. They also help us stay ourselves.

Finally, the simplest move: get thoughts out of your head.

Holding an idea in mind while simultaneously trying to work with it is expensive. Once it’s written down, it becomes external — something you can look at rather than strain to hold. You no longer need to remember it; you can think about it instead. A completed concept map makes knowledge visible. You can see the whole at once.

Sometimes the most productive tool is the most ordinary: a pencil, a notebook, a pair of eyes.

### Distributed Cognition

Start with an underrated ability: imitation.

Imitators can use others as filters, selecting effective paths from a field of options. They can draw from a range of solutions and adapt as conditions change. They avoid repeating mistakes that have already been made.

The clearest example is Jobs. In 1979 he visited Xerox PARC and saw early prototypes of the graphical interface and mouse. He didn’t copy what he saw. He went back and analyzed why those solutions worked, identified how Apple’s situation differed, and adapted the ideas to fit the personal computer market. The successful imitator, it turns out, is a kind of innovator.

Imitation is learning from others. The next step is thinking _with_ them.

An overlooked fact: intelligent thinking is, at its root, a social process. Humans are not especially good at thinking in abstractions. We are exceptionally good at thinking about people. Instead of transmitting knowledge as concepts, put it into people and stories.

Research shows we recall about 50% more from stories than from expository text. Reading a story, the brain runs a simulation — it generates an internal film of the events. The story becomes a form of vicarious practice: we rehearse experiences that haven’t happened to us.

Output reverses this direction. Teaching is a highly social act — it triggers cognitive and attentional processes that change the teacher’s own thinking. Studies show that explaining something on camera creates a genuine sense of _social presence_ , raising physiological arousal and with it memory and focus. If you want to really understand something, teaching it is one of the fastest routes.

Then there’s debate. Genuine argument forces us to occupy the position of someone evaluating another’s view, not just promoting our own.

> Argue as if you are right. Listen as if you are wrong.

Wider still: collective thinking. Cognitive anthropologist Edwin Hutchins studied what he called _socially distributed cognition_ — the way people think using the ideas of others. He argued that cognition doesn’t stop at the individual — a group can function as a single thinking system. Psychologist Daniel Wegner called a related phenomenon _transactive memory_. In close relationships and stable teams, people divide up what they remember — each person holds different information, and together they exceed what any one of them could manage alone.

But group thinking has a persistent failure mode: discussion is dominated by a few voices, and members tend to share information everyone already knows, while keeping their unique knowledge to themselves. A simple fix: have people write down their ideas rather than speak them. Or have the group leader stay silent at the start, making space for others to go first.

In activities where people move in sync, the boundary between self and other blurs — not into smallness, but into expansion. Studies of athletes and dancers find that synchronized movement increases endurance and reduces the perception of physical pain.

The focus of thought can shift from _I_ to  _we_.

We have always thought with the whole world. We just rarely notice.

Beyond the brain — the body, the space, the people around us — all of it already is.
