Three art students, sitting in different exam rooms, each draw a set of meaningless geometric shapes, none of them knowing what they’re drawing. When the drawings are later assembled, they form a bee.

This is a scene from _Black Gate_ (黑门, 2022), a Chinese animated series set in 2058, where brain-computer interfaces have become as ubiquitous as the internet. The scene occurs immediately after a system malfunction disconnects all BCI users simultaneously. Thought-data begins to scramble across the network. A shared model emerges across distributed minds, and “swarm syndrome” begins to spread.

The first time I watched it, I took it as science fiction. The second time, Kevin Kelly came to mind.

When _Black Gate_ aired in 2022, ChatGPT was still about six months away from entering public awareness, and brain-computer interfaces were far from mainstream discourse. After watching the series, I went on to pick up Kelly’s _Out of Control_. It is now 2026. Large language models have gone through several major iterations. Neuralink’s first human trial participants can already control a cursor, perform selections, and complete simple digital tasks.

Rereading Kevin Kelly’s trilogy (_Out of Control_ , _What Technology Wants_ , _The Inevitable_) and rewatching _Black Gate_ recently, I kept noticing patterns I had missed before.

### Out of Control

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*uRbn63hTpNyM1EeJLkdRQw.png)

The bee drawing captures a central idea in Kelly’s first book: hive mind thinking.

What Kelly is really getting at is not bees themselves. He uses the hive as a model for a broader class of systems. Individuals operate with only local information, yet intelligence emerges at the level of the whole. He calls these “living systems.”

Three counterintuitive ideas:

**Intelligence doesn’t need a center.** The queen does not direct the hive; she lays eggs. Collective behaviors such as migration, temperature regulation, and nest construction emerge from thousands of bees following simple local rules. Complex intelligence can arise from interaction without centralized control or planning.

**Looser control often produces stronger adaptability.** Centrally controlled systems may be efficient in stable environments, but they are vulnerable when conditions shift abruptly. A single failure in the command chain can cascade through the entire system. A hive has no single point of failure. For Kelly, this is the core advantage of decentralized systems.

**The whole is real, but it can’t be located.** The intelligence of the hive does not reside in any single bee or any specific place, yet it is observable and operational. This challenges a common assumption that function must be tied to a specific origin. Instead, function can be distributed across relationships rather than nodes.

The hive is only Kelly’s entry point. What he is ultimately exploring is what happens when increasingly complex systems — ecosystems, economies, the internet, artificial intelligence — are allowed to operate without tight control. His answer is emergent intelligence, paired with irreducible unpredictability. “Out of control” is not a failure state but a structural property of living systems.

_Out of Control_ was published in 1994. Over the following three decades, many of the systems Kelly described have, to varying degrees, materialized in the real world.

### What Technology Wants

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*MwLOQkChY0mVHfpR-lB4Uw.png)

In _Black Gate_ , the antagonist argues that technology drives human evolution, and that a new form of humanity is inevitable. His goals are arguable; his methods are not. But the underlying intuition is one Kevin Kelly takes seriously in _What Technology Wants_.

Kelly suggests that technology can be understood as evolving in ways similar to biological life, with its own directional tendencies toward diversity, complexity, and the relaxation of constraints. He calls this force the “technium”: the superorganism formed by humans, their tools, systems, and cultural and technological networks.

A few of its core propositions:

**The technium has evolutionary momentum.** Certain technologies appear to arise independently across different civilizations — the telephone, calculus, the airplane — suggesting a form of technological convergence rather than purely contingent invention.

**Technology wants more complexity, diversity, and autonomy.** This is not because it is conscious, but because the long arc of technological evolution consistently moves toward higher connectivity, expanded possibility, and increased structural complexity. That directionality is itself a kind of “want.”

**The relationship between humans and technology is symbiotic rather than hierarchical.** Technology shapes human cognition and desire, while humans simultaneously steer technological development. Without technology, “humans” in the modern sense would not exist. Resistance is possible, as Kelly’s discussion of the Amish illustrates, but it does not break the co-evolutionary loop.

Seen this way, the antagonist in _Black Gate_ is not simply a villain with a plan. He is someone who has fully internalized the idea that technological evolution is not optional. The emergence of a new kind of human, in his view, is not a choice but a trajectory.

### The Inevitable

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*UR5G7mR2uj8Wl6xZgRemGQ.png)

 _The Inevitable_ was published in 2016, before ChatGPT and before large language models became part of everyday infrastructure. Kelly argues that the next thirty years of technological change can be understood structurally. He frames this through twelve verbs, emphasizing process over state: becoming, cognifying, flowing, screening, accessing, sharing, filtering, remixing, interacting, tracking, questioning, and beginning.

Several of his observations now read with unusual clarity in hindsight.

**Becoming is a permanent condition.** No system or product is ever truly finished; everything exists in continuous update. Stability is an illusion, and flux is the default state.

**Cognification will permeate infrastructure.** Artificial intelligence, he suggests, will become embedded in everything much like electricity — less a product than a general-purpose capability.

**Ownership gradually gives way to access.** Across many domains, the ability to use matters more than possession. This shift reflects deeper structural changes in technological economies.

**Filtering becomes more important than information itself.** In environments saturated with data, attention becomes the scarce resource. Control over filtering mechanisms increasingly implies control over cognition itself.

In _Black Gate_ , the brain-computer system has become a layer of cognitive infrastructure and, ultimately, a mode of production. If the trajectories Kelly described in 2016 extend forward another forty years, this outcome is not speculative so much as extrapolative.

### Redefining “Human”

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*AmC97MQM-eFPYHNpW5dcUQ.png)

Near the end of _Black Gate_ , a superintelligent AI invokes two frameworks that have historically been used to define the human mind: Plato’s tripartite soul — appetite, spirit, reason — and Stanovich’s tripartite model of cognition — autonomous mind, algorithmic mind, reflective mind.

The question is explicit: if the consciousness of swarm synchronization begins to merge across individuals, do the affected subjects still qualify as human under either framework? Or do they exceed the conceptual boundaries those frameworks were designed to describe?

This is not a question Kelly’s trilogy directly addresses. His work remains at the level of systems, trajectories, and evolutionary pressures. Yet the question sits beneath all three books. As artificial intelligence enters cognition and brain-computer interfaces begin to connect minds, the boundary between human and technological systems becomes increasingly unstable.

_Out of Control_ establishes the structural logic. _What Technology Wants_ extends it into evolutionary time. _The Inevitable_ renders it as a set of observable processes. Together, they describe how systems evolve.

As _The Inevitable_ concludes: it has already begun. Though, of course, that is only the beginning.
