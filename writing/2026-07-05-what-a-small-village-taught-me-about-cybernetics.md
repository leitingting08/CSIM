_“We are but whirlpools in a river of ever-flowing water. We are not stuff that abides, but patterns that perpetuate themselves.”_ — Norbert Wiener, The Human Use of Human Beings.

I came back to Dali wanting to make dumplings.

Three years ago, I’d gone to the town market with some friends from the neighborhood and picked up dumpling wrappers without a second thought — one of those small, unremarkable errands. This time, I spent twenty minutes wandering the same market, asking seven or eight vendors, and came up empty. I eventually ordered them through a grocery delivery app.

It was a small thing. But it made me start paying attention.

Over the next few days, I noticed other shifts: the weekly market had expanded, with more stalls and more foot traffic, yet somehow the basics felt harder to find. The lamb rice noodle shop I used to love was shuttered, mid-renovation. And at the entrance to the village, the old banyan tree — the one that had probably been there for centuries — was now ringed with a red wooden fence and a sign that read “Wishing Tree.”

Same village. Different texture. I kept feeling like these changes were connected, though I couldn’t quite say how.

I’d been reading Norbert Wiener’s _Cybernetics_ , and it gave me a way in.

The word comes from the Greek _kybernetes_ — a ship’s helmsman. The helmsman doesn’t force the river to change course. He reads the current, adjusts constantly, and keeps the vessel on track through a continuous loop of perception and correction. That’s what control means in Wiener’s framework: not top-down force, but self-regulation through feedback.

Which brings up a question: what determines what information a system receives in the first place? Attention does. Attention is itself a kind of language — it decides what gets read, and what gets ignored.

### Information and Feedback: How a System Knows Itself

The core mechanism in cybernetics is the feedback loop. A system acts, senses the result, and adjusts. A thermostat does this. So does a body maintaining its temperature, or a person learning to ride a bike.

The whole thing depends on accurate information flowing through the loop. Cut that off, and the feedback fails. One way to think about addiction, for instance, is that it hijacks normal feedback pathways — the body keeps receiving signals to continue while remaining unable to register the real cost.

This village ran on a clear feedback loop for generations. Fishing on Erhai Lake, reading the catch, adjusting the nets, passing the knowledge on. The loop was tight, the transmission nearly lossless. It didn’t need to be designed or maintained — it just ran.

Then the government designated Erhai as a protected lake, restricting fishing to specific seasons. The information channel that had organized life here for generations was partially severed. The system lost its way of calibrating itself, and had to start looking for new signals.

### Homeostasis and Disruption: Why the Old Order Held So Long

Cybernetics adopted the biological concept of homeostasis — the capacity of a system to maintain its balance in the face of external disruption. Homeostasis isn’t stillness; it’s dynamic equilibrium, like a tightrope walker who looks motionless but is constantly making micro-adjustments.

The Chinese sociologist Fei Xiaotong wrote about something similar in the context of rural life. In _From the Soil_ , his most widely read work, he sketched the specific shape of that continuity in rural China: land, kinship, customary order, passed down through practice rather than instruction. A child learns to fish by watching a parent fish. Knowledge moves through daily life, not classrooms. In a lesser-known companion volume, _The Institution of Reproduction_ , he described this more broadly as the continuity of society — the way a society perpetuates itself through its institutions without anyone needing to consciously design the process.

From a cybernetic standpoint, this is an extremely efficient feedback system. Information travels short distances, between people who share context, with minimal loss between generations. The system sustains itself without external input.

It held as long as the environment didn’t produce disruptions it couldn’t absorb. The fishing restrictions were a disruption of a different magnitude. A feedback loop that had run for generations broke down almost overnight. The system was forced off its old equilibrium and had to find a new one.

Young people leaving for the cities: the system contracting. A few returning to open guesthouses: new feedback loops being tested. The village committee redesigning the market, putting up the Wishing Tree sign, organizing bonfires to draw tourists: someone trying — in the language of systems theory — to engineer a new attractor, to pull the system toward a tourism-based equilibrium.

Whether a designed equilibrium can hold the way an organic one did — that’s still an open question.

### Fast Variables and Slow Variables: What Changes, What Doesn’t

Not everything in a system moves at the same speed.

Systems theorists distinguish between fast variables and slow variables. Fast variables respond quickly to new signals: market demand, a shop’s business model, the number of guesthouses in a village. These can shift visibly within a few years.

Slow variables barely seem to move at all: geography, climate, the accumulated embodied knowledge of generations, and a banyan tree that has been growing for several hundred years. These form the underlying constraints within which fast variables fluctuate.

The dumpling wrapper stall disappearing — that’s a fast variable. A small supplier couldn’t adapt to the new demand structure and quietly exited. The banyan tree still standing where it has always stood, roots pushing further into the ground — that’s a slow variable. It doesn’t respond to quarterly market shifts. It just keeps growing.

The village committee’s intervention is interesting precisely because of this gap. Putting a red wooden fence around the tree, hanging a “Wishing Tree” sign — that’s wrapping a fast variable (a new symbol, a new commercial logic) around a slow one (the tree itself). Trying to wrap a new commercial logic around a centuries-old spiritual focal point. The fence can go up. The sign can be hung. But the tree doesn’t change because of the sign. Slow variables have their own timescale.

I mentioned this to a friend in Fujian, where I visited earlier this year. There was a large banyan at the entrance to his village too — and another in the village where I grew up, which people used to worship before the local temple was ever built. We started comparing notes and realized: almost every village seems to have one. Sometimes as an object of faith, sometimes as a meeting point, sometimes as both.

The function is remarkably consistent across regions and cultures — a node of belief, a hub for information, a center of public life. There’s something operating here that’s older and more durable than any specific ritual or custom. A collective intuition about where the center is. It has been running for centuries, expressed in different languages in different eras.

### Second-Order Cybernetics: The Observer Is Inside the System

Later developments in cybernetics led to what Heinz von Foerster called second-order cybernetics. First-order cybernetics studies observed systems from the outside, assuming the observer’s neutrality. Second-order cybernetics turns that assumption over: the observer is always inside the system. There is no genuinely neutral vantage point. When you observe a system, that act of observation is itself part of what’s happening.

I’ve been coming to this village since 2023. What I noticed depended entirely on what I brought with me.

In 2023, I was paying attention to food, prices, where to spend time. I wasn’t looking for anything structural. This time, I came with Wiener and Fei Xiaotong still in my head, and I found myself noticing the missing dumpling wrappers, the shuttered rice noodle shop, the fenced-in tree. Those details were all there before. I just wasn’t treating them as signals.

This points to something deeper than “different frameworks reveal different things.” The filter through which we receive information is itself part of the system. Attention shapes what information becomes available. Information determines what feedback is possible. Feedback determines what we do next. If you don’t know a certain signal exists in a system, you will never respond to it.

The rice noodle shop that closed — in a sense, this is exactly that. It slipped out of people’s field of attention long before it disappeared from the street. Nobody recorded its decline. By the time anyone thought to look for it, it was already gone.

Things that vanish from a system are often invisible long before they actually disappear. Cybernetics offers something like a pair of glasses for seeing this: when a system is running well, it tends to be invisible, the way you don’t notice the air. It’s only when a feedback loop breaks down, when something you took for granted suddenly isn’t there, that you realize it had been holding something together all along.

Not being able to find dumpling wrappers in a market where you bought them easily three years ago is that kind of signal.

Small. Easy to miss. Worth paying attention to.
