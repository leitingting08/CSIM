> “The power of reasons is an illusion. The belief will not change when the reasons are defeated. The causality is reversed. People believe the reasons because they believe in the conclusion.” — Daniel Kahneman

In _Thinking, Fast and Slow_ , Daniel Kahneman splits the mind into two systems.

System 1 is fast. It runs automatically, effortlessly, and you can’t turn it off. You see an angry face and instantly know the person is upset. No deliberation required.

System 2 is slow. It demands attention, drains energy, and defaults to doing as little as possible. Solving a complex math problem, focusing on one voice in a noisy room, resisting an impulse purchase — all System 2 tasks.

System 1 constantly feeds impressions, intuitions, and impulses to System 2. When System 2 endorses them, impressions become beliefs and impulses become actions. The problem is that System 2 defaults to low effort. Most of the time it’s loosely monitoring System 1’s output, not actively auditing it. So System 1’s suggestions often go through without review. System 2 only fully engages when it encounters surprise, contradiction, or an explicit demand to think harder.

### Prospect theory: how people actually respond to gains and losses

In 1979, Kahneman and Amos Tversky proposed prospect theory, directly challenging economics’ model of _Homo Economicus_ — the assumption that people make rational calculations under uncertainty. This was the core contribution behind Kahneman’s 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics. What it revealed is that human responses to gains and losses follow their own logic, one that looks nothing like rational optimisation.

Prospect theory rests on three mechanisms.

**Reference dependence.** People judge outcomes not by their absolute value but by how they compare to a reference point. Having a million dollars feels completely different depending on whether you got there from five hundred thousand or fell there from two million. Same outcome, different reference point, opposite emotional response.

**Loss aversion.** The pain of losing something is roughly twice the pleasure of gaining the same thing. This is why most people reject a bet with a 50% chance of winning 200 and a 50% chance of losing 100 — even though the expected value is positive. People aren’t maximising gains. They’re minimising the feeling of loss.

**Diminishing sensitivity.** The psychological impact of gains and losses isn’t linear. Going from a small loss to a moderate one hurts sharply; going from a huge loss to a slightly larger one barely registers. The same holds for gains — the jump from 0 to 1,000 feels bigger than from 1,000 to 2,000. Plot this on a curve and you get an asymmetric S-shape, steeper on the loss side.

These three mechanisms, layered together, explain a lot of seemingly irrational behaviour.

Retail investors are a textbook case. They hold losing positions and sell winning ones. Holding a loser avoids turning a paper loss into a realized loss — loss aversion at work. Selling a winner locks in profit before the reference point shifts — the anchor has already moved from “what I paid” to “what it’s worth now.” As a result, they systematically pour resources into failing assets and cut short the ones that are working. The investor isn’t evaluating the asset. They’re reacting to whether the number on the screen says red or green.

Sometimes the most painful outcome isn’t the worst one. It’s the zone where things almost turned out fine.

### Loss aversion and narrow framing

Loss aversion is everywhere once you start looking. Consider spending an hour hunting for something you don’t need just to use up a coupon. The unused coupon is a concrete, visible loss signal. The wasted hour is abstract and invisible. System 1 is highly sensitive to the former and barely registers the latter.

Loss aversion often arrives with narrow framing — the tendency to treat each decision as an isolated event rather than part of a larger picture. Tracking every trade’s profit and loss individually means getting jerked around by each fluctuation. Stepping back to evaluate the portfolio’s long-term performance produces better decisions. But System 1 defaults to case-by-case processing. Switching to a broad frame requires System 2 to step in.

Narrow framing shows up in relationships too. One cold response, one careless remark, one rejection — and the conclusion is that the other person dislikes you. But a single data point is noise. The signal is in the long-term pattern.

Broad framing is the opposite: looking at the whole, the long run, the portfolio, the probabilities. Instead of fixating on a single event, place it back in the broader context where it belongs.

### Heuristics and biases: System 1’s shortcuts and their costs

Prospect theory describes how people react to gains and losses. The other half of Kahneman’s Nobel-winning work asks a different question: when people judge probabilities and make predictions, what method are they actually using?

The answer is heuristics — mental shortcuts that System 1 runs automatically. They’re fast and usually good enough, but under specific conditions they produce systematic errors.

**Anchoring** is one. When making a judgement, people unconsciously latch onto whatever number or piece of information appeared first, and subsequent estimates drift around that anchor. The original price tag in a store is an anchor — the discount feels like a bargain because the sticker price has already framed your sense of value. There’s a subtler version: you don’t mind paying a certain price until you learn a friend got the same item cheaper at the same store. Nothing about the product has changed. The money is already spent. But a new anchor has recalibrated your judgement.

**Representativeness** is another shortcut. People judge the probability that something belongs to a category based on how much it resembles that category, ignoring base rates. Kahneman’s famous experiment: subjects are told “Linda cares about social justice and studied philosophy,” then asked whether she’s more likely to be a bank teller or a bank teller who is also a feminist. Most pick the latter — but the latter is a subset of the former, so its probability can only be lower. System 1 finds the description so similar to “feminist” that resemblance overrides probability.

Then there’s **availability** : people estimate how likely an event is based on how easily examples come to mind. Plane crashes get dense media coverage and vivid footage, so people overestimate the risk. Strokes kill far more people but rarely make the news, so the risk feels low. If you can picture it, it must be common — that’s System 1’s default logic.

What these shortcuts share is that they work well in most everyday situations but fail systematically under specific conditions — and the direction of failure is predictable.

### Two selves: what you lived and what you remember are not the same thing

The latter part of the book introduces a concept I think is underappreciated: the experiencing self and the remembering self.

The experiencing self is the one living your life right now — feeling things moment to moment. The remembering self is the one that looks back, keeps score, makes choices, and tells the story.

These two selves frequently disagree. The remembering self has two biases: the peak-end rule and duration neglect. The peak-end rule means it evaluates an experience mainly by two moments — the most intense point and the ending — not by the average quality of the whole thing. Duration neglect means it barely cares how long the experience lasted.

Together, these biases create a counterintuitive tendency: people prefer intense short-term pleasure over mild long-term contentment. A brief but vivid experience can outrank a long stretch of quiet happiness in memory. The reverse holds too — people fear brief, sharp pain more than moderate chronic suffering, because the latter has no peak for the remembering self to register.

Think about what this explains. Why do some people stay in relationships that slowly drain them day after day (moderate long-term pain, no peak, the remembering self thinks “it’s not that bad”), yet dread the acute pain of a breakup? Why do others keep chasing novelty (brief, intense peaks) while losing interest in stable daily life (steady but peakless — nothing for the remembering self to score)?

The remembering self also edits the past. Not because things were actually better, but because the editing process unconsciously trims away the flat and unpleasant parts, leaving only the peaks. “The good old days” almost always feel better than they actually were. The past didn’t improve. The remembering self just runs post-production.

When the remembering self drives decisions, much of what we choose isn’t optimised for living well. It’s optimised for remembering well.

### Cognitive restructuring

If the previous article on _The Path of Least Resistance_ was about how structural tension drives behaviour, this one is about recognising your own cognitive system — seeing reality more clearly so that structural tension actually points toward your vision.

System 1 and System 2 need to work together. System 1 is fast but uncontrollable. System 2 is slow but capable of depth. The key insight is that System 2’s work can feed back into System 1.

Language learning is a clear example. Early on, System 2 does everything — translating word by word, recalling grammar rules, assembling sentences consciously. It’s slow and exhausting. But with enough practice, you stop translating and start thinking in the new language. That skill has migrated from System 2 to System 1. It’s become automatic.

The same applies to the two selves. Once you understand the peak-end rule, you can recognise why the past always seems better than the present — that’s not fact, that’s the remembering self running post-production. Once you understand duration neglect, you can pause before a decision and ask: am I choosing for the experiencing self or the remembering self? These insights won’t eliminate the biases, but they create a window for System 2 to intervene before System 1 locks in its answer.

Cognitive restructuring works the same way. Through reading, thinking, observing, and repeated testing, System 2 examines System 1’s default patterns and gradually updates its model of the world.

Anchoring and loss aversion won’t disappear. But when you practise broad framing repeatedly, it begins to become your default response. When you regularly catch anchoring effects, your sensitivity to anchors rises naturally. What System 2 does deliberately today becomes what System 1 does automatically tomorrow. This isn’t a one-time fix. It’s an ongoing process — using slow thinking to refine fast reactions, one adjustment at a time.
