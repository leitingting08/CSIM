_Information itself is the reward_

Lately I’ve been watching myself watch things. I’ve noticed that over the past few years, I’ve grown increasingly drawn to randomness. When I land in an unfamiliar city, I rarely plan a route — I’d rather just wander and see what I come across.

Recently I rejoined a friend’s project: identifying one new plant every day. What draws me in, I think, isn’t really “getting to know plants.” It’s not knowing, each morning, which one it’ll be — some plant I’ve walked past a hundred times without ever really looking at. There’s a small thrill in that: a little unplanned increment of information, arriving at random, every single day.

I like reading across disciplines, and I like watching things change. Even when I write, more often than not I don’t start with a question or an answer — I let random collisions spark something, then follow wherever it leads.

So I got curious: why am I so drawn to randomness in the first place? And is there anything that actually explains it?

### Unexpected things carry more information

Claude Shannon’s information theory, proposed in 1948, gives a precise definition of “information”: the less probable an event is, the more information it carries. The formula is simple — information equals the negative logarithm of probability. Flip a fair coin and get heads, and that’s 1 bit of information. Flip a rigged coin that lands heads 90% of the time, and heads only carries about 0.15 bits — because you’d already half-expected it. Something certain — the sun rising tomorrow, for someone who’s sure it will — carries virtually no information at all.

By that logic, the more surprising something is, the more information it holds. If your itinerary says “arrive at 10am” and you arrive at 10am, that event carries almost no information — it just confirms what you already knew. But an unexpected rainstorm, or a meal you never planned to have, carries far more, precisely because it was improbable, unscheduled. This seems to explain why the unplanned moments are the ones we remember.

But that explanation isn’t enough. Pure randomness, it turns out, is what we forget fastest.

If more information meant more memorable, then a maximum-entropy random signal — white noise — should be the most captivating thing there is. It’s the opposite. When every instant of a signal is completely unpredictable, when entropy approaches its maximum, we can no longer extract any structure from it at all.

Information is not the same thing as randomness. Information describes how much surprise a single event delivers; randomness describes how uncertain an entire system is, on average. The two are closely related, but not the same thing.

Which means information content alone can’t explain why I’m drawn to randomness. Something’s missing.

### What’s actually doing the work is an inverted U

In the 1960s and 70s, psychologist Daniel Berlyne proposed that aesthetic pleasure and stimulus complexity trace an inverted-U curve: things that are too simple, too predictable, bore us; things too complex, too chaotic, overwhelm us and feel unpleasant. What we find genuinely pleasurable, genuinely memorable, sits in the middle — a pattern faintly visible, but not obvious at a glance. What captivates us, in other words, isn’t pure randomness, but a state suspended between order and chaos.

Later researchers, working within the framework of predictive coding, offered a more granular account: the brain is constantly predicting what happens next, and both our pleasure and how well we remember something track how far reality deviates from that prediction. Match the prediction exactly, and the brain sees nothing worth processing. Deviate completely, and the brain can’t find any pattern to hold onto, and gives up just the same. What gets deeply processed — and, as a result, remembered — is that narrow middle ground: something with a discernible pattern, but not one you can fully guess. This moderate level of prediction error is precisely what sustains attention and drives deeper processing, and that’s what makes it stick in memory.

Neuroscience research has found that moderate surprise and curiosity activate the brain’s dopamine-driven reward circuit — a loop running through the ventral tegmental area, the nucleus accumbens, and the hippocampus. Once triggered, this circuit doesn’t just produce pleasure; it directly strengthens memory encoding and consolidation. What we’re hooked on isn’t information itself, but the act of acquiring it.

### So What Do We Mean by “Random”?

Following the inverted-U curve back to where we started, it turns out that “unplanned moments are more memorable” can come from two structurally different things.

The first is a plan getting disrupted: you were supposed to arrive somewhere at 10am, but the rain sent you somewhere else instead. Here, a specific expectation existed first, and reality later diverged from it — the “surprise” is defined relative to that expectation.

But there’s a second structure, one that works differently: you know where you’re headed, but you deliberately refuse to imagine what will happen once you get there. You throw yourself into a field left open to any possibility. In this case, no specific expectation was ever built in the first place, only to be broken later.

Information theory has a name for exactly this second case. Physicist Edwin Jaynes, in 1957, proposed the principle of maximum entropy: faced with a situation where you hold only limited, known constraints, the least biased choice is the distribution with maximum entropy. Don’t assume anything you don’t actually know. Put plainly: acknowledge only what you know, and refuse to write the ending for everything else in advance.

“I know where I’m going, but I won’t think about what happens once I’m there” does exactly this: it sets the destination as the only known constraint, and leaves every other dimension open, without adding a single extra assumption. What keeps a field genuinely fertile is setting the fewest constraints possible, and then, for everything that doesn’t have to be assumed — simply not assuming it.

Which means what moves us, what we remember, doesn’t necessarily require a specific expectation to be broken. As long as there are one or two real anchors in the background — a destination, a stretch of time, a self that’s actually present — the rest can be left open, honestly.

Looking at it this way, “I’m drawn to randomness” might not be quite the right way to put it. A more accurate version might be: what I’m drawn to is leaving myself one or two real anchors, and refusing to write the script for everything unknown in advance. The more honestly you leave things open, the closer what the world gives back comes to what it actually is.
