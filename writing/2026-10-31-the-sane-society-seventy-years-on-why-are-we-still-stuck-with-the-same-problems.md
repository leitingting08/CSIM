_“The danger of the past was that men became slaves. The danger of the future is that men may become robots.” — Erich Fromm, The Sane Society_

I recently finished Erich Fromm’s _The Sane Society_ , reading it in the gaps of a busy semester.

More than seventy years have passed since it came out. Society has changed enormously, technology keeps advancing, and the way we live keeps updating itself. Yet some of the problems Fromm described don’t seem to have gone away. If anything, they have dug in deeper, in new forms.

The questions he raised still hold. Which leaves me wondering: when a social pathology has been identified and discussed for more than seventy years, why is it still here?

### The Pathology of Normalcy

We usually judge whether a person is healthy by whether they can adapt to society: hold down a job, maintain relationships, finish on time what others expect them to finish. The one who can’t adapt is the one who needs treatment.

Fromm thought this standard was itself the problem. It assumes society is normal: as long as society keeps running, any failure to fit in can only be an individual defect. He called this position sociological relativism. Society is the yardstick; people can be measured against it, but they can never turn around and measure the yardstick.

He asked the question in reverse: **could a society itself be insane?**

At the start of the book, Fromm cites some unsettling statistics: in some of the richest, most democratic and most peaceful Western countries of his time, rates of alcoholism and suicide were among the highest. Material conditions had improved; people’s inner lives had not followed.

In his view, human beings have certain basic needs that don’t change from one society to the next: to relate to others, to feel like the author of their own actions, to have an identity, to be rooted somewhere, to make sense of the world they live in. Whether a society is sane depends not on how smoothly it runs, but on whether it meets these needs.

> _Just as there is a_ folie à deux _, there is a_ folie à millions _. The fact that millions of people share the same vices does not make these vices virtues, the fact that they share so many errors does not make the errors to be truths, and the fact that millions of people share the same forms of mental pathology does not make these people sane._

What cuts deepest here is this: **the hardest illness to notice is the one everyone has.** A person who feels out of place on their own will suspect something is wrong with them. But when everyone lives the same way, that sense of wrongness can become the norm. Society even supplies ready-made explanations and exits for it — entertainment, consumption, busyness — so that no one has to really face the emptiness.

Fromm also took aim at how mass media were eroding people’s inner lives:

> _We have radio, television, movies, a newspaper a day for everybody. But instead of giving us the best of past and present literature and music, these media of communication supplemented by advertising fill the minds of men with the cheapest trash, lacking in any sense of reality, with sadistic phantasies._

Later, Neil Postman carried this critique into the age of television and technology, which I wrote about in an earlier essay. Over seventy years the media have changed several times, but their function seems unchanged: to make the emptiness bearable.

So the best-adjusted person is not necessarily the sanest one. They may simply be the one who has internalized a socially patterned defect most completely, so completely they can no longer feel it. Which brings us to the question: **if a society leaves most of its people less than sane, whose problem is that?** Fromm’s answer is clear. It is not only the individual’s problem; it is society’s.

### The Problem That Was Seen

If Fromm pointed all this out seventy years ago, why hasn’t it gone away?

One straightforward explanation: knowing how a mechanism works is not the same as being able to step out of it. We can see clearly that we are being shaped by systems of evaluation, and still need grades, jobs and income. We can notice that busyness is wearing us down, and still be afraid to stop. Structures don’t loosen just because they’ve been seen through.

But I think there is a subtler reason, one Fromm had already explored in his earlier book, _Escape from Freedom_.

In January I wrote an essay on that book. What moved me then was his account of the predicament of freedom: people throw off external constraints, but don’t necessarily gain the capacity to act and create independently. The loneliness and uncertainty that freedom brings can be hard to bear. So people find their way back to some kind of security — through submitting to authority, through automaton conformity. Conformity is especially hard to notice: becoming what others expect, dissolving your particularity into sameness with those around you.

Reading the two books together, I realized that what traps people is not only external structure, but their reliance on it. Once you see a mechanism clearly, actually leaving it means facing the uncertainty of choice again, answering on your own what you want and how you should live. Conformity at least offers a ready-made answer.

And so the problem is seen, but not necessarily resisted. We see it, and we stay.

### A Self-Correcting System

I once came to understand cybernetics through a village: when a system is disturbed, it adjusts itself through feedback and returns to a stable state. At the time I found this self-correction beautiful. It is what makes a system resilient.

Reading _The Sane Society_ , I saw the other side of the same mechanism for the first time: **a system’s self-correction serves its own stability first, and does not necessarily point toward human well-being.**

For a society, Fromm’s critique was itself a disturbance. It wasn’t suppressed; it was absorbed. Once alienation had been diagnosed, the system’s response was not to change the conditions that produced it, but to offer products that relieve it: mindfulness courses, self-care, energy management, productivity methods. Each has genuinely helped some people. But they all share one premise: the one who needs adjusting is you.

Fromm’s own thinking went through the same process. A year after _The Sane Society_ , he published _The Art of Loving_ , which became his most widely read book; I wrote about it in August. What people remember is that love is a skill that has to be practiced. What is rarely mentioned is another judgment in the book, just as explicit: the principles on which modern society runs are at odds with love. In such a society, mature love can hardly become a widespread experience; if that is to change, society itself has to change.

Fromm said this was society’s problem. Today we have grown used to answering: it’s my problem, I need to adjust. The problem hasn’t been solved. It has been redistributed to each of us.

### Alienation Turned Inward

My first impression was that the alienation Fromm described is accelerating. But having come this far, I want to be more precise: perhaps what has changed is not the degree of alienation, but where it lives.

What Fromm called the “marketing orientation” is the way people come to experience themselves as commodities: their value depends not on what they are, but on whether they can sell. In 1955, this evaluation showed up more through external market relations — employers, the market, the gaze of others — but it was already becoming the way people perceived themselves.

> _Since he experiences himself as a thing, an investment, to be manipulated by himself and by others, he is lacking in a sense of self. This lack of self creates deep anxiety._

Today, external evaluation seems to have moved further into how we see ourselves. Step counts, sleep scores, screen time, books read, follower counts — almost every part of life can be quantified, compared and optimized. The metrics themselves aren’t the problem. But once they become the measure of our worth, we are no longer just recording our lives; we are continuously grading ourselves by an external standard.

No boss needs to be in the room: we score ourselves. No one needs to ask: we treat ourselves as a project to be managed. Byung-Chul Han later named this condition, in _The Burnout Society_ , the “achievement society”: exploiter and exploited become the same person. Pressure no longer mainly takes the form of “you must” but of “you can.” Believing our possibilities are limitless, we keep demanding that we do more and become better. Even stopping is easily read as not trying hard enough.

So perhaps the acceleration is not only that we are under more pressure, but that pressure has learned to drive itself. We have learned to monitor ourselves, assess ourselves, push ourselves — and to call all of it self-discipline and growth.

### The Limits of the Personal

This brings me back to my January essay on _Escape from Freedom_. Back then I was stuck in a feeling that nothing was worth writing, and the answer I gave myself was: admit that feeling, stop trying to be seen as profound, return to writing itself, to spontaneous activity. Write what I want to write, and nothing more.

Rereading it now, I still agree with that answer. But I can also see its limit: it was an answer that happened only within me. Faced with the question Fromm raised, I did what most people do: I folded it back into myself and made a correction at the personal level.

That doesn’t make personal awareness wrong. But I notice that the way out Fromm describes goes well beyond adjusting oneself:

> _If man lives under conditions which are contrary to his nature and to the basic requirements for human growth and sanity, he cannot help reacting; he must either degenerate and perish, or create conditions which are more in accordance with his needs._

His answer is not to adjust, but to **create conditions**.

Which means that when the conditions of life keep running against people’s basic needs, the way out cannot only be making ourselves better able to tolerate them. Adjusting ourselves may ease the conflict, but it can also get us used to things we should never have grown used to. Personal awareness is not the whole answer, but it is part of it.

At the very least, it can change where we locate the problem. When I feel exhausted, hollow, or vaguely sense that something is off, I no longer have to assume right away that I’m not good enough, not disciplined enough, not adjusting well enough. Perhaps part of the problem was never mine alone to carry.
