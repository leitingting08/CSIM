> “Our experience is always changing and, furthermore, is always dependent on a particular situation. To be human, indeed to be living, is always to be in a situation, a context, a world. We have no experience of anything that is permanent and independent of these situations.” — Varela, Thompson & Rosch, The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience

![](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*9Z81_frtr24WU2roZ6SHzA.png)

After two months of summer in Dali, I packed my bags within a week and left for Barcelona.

If I hadn’t already booked the flight and locked in a departure date, I might not have been able to bring myself to leave Dali at all. Two months had been enough to build a kind of living momentum, and right before leaving, I still felt that familiar mix of fear and excitement that comes with facing something new and unknown.

Before the move, my luggage went through a round of decluttering. Then, to survive an entire year in an unfamiliar climate and country, it swelled right back up again, stuffed with every kind of “just in case.”

### The Body Before the Head

In a familiar environment, we tend to underestimate how much we actually depend on. Moving between places so frequently made me realize again that so-called “living momentum” isn’t just a psychological habit. The body remembers which route to take each day, where to eat, when to go out, what temperature calls for what clothes, which sounds it can relax around, which people I don’t need to rehearse myself around. Normally, none of this ever rises into awareness — until you change places, and one by one, it all stops working, and suddenly becomes visible.

Changing cities, especially changing countries, means the body has to relearn an entire way of living.

Sleep comes first. Because of the time difference, I got sleepy absurdly early in the first few days, and woke absurdly early too. It took over a week for my body to slowly find its old rhythm again. My mind knew the time difference was only a few hours — but my body had to recalculate, from scratch, what “night” even meant, what “time to sleep” felt like.

Then comes food. Switching from Chinese food to Western food isn’t just a matter of taste. What produce the local supermarket carries, what’s in the kitchen, what spices are within reach, and how much time I’m actually willing to spend cooking — all of it quietly decides what I eat every day.

So the eating patterns I once knew stopped working, and I found myself constantly searching for a new path of least resistance: what’s easy to buy, what’s easy to make, what combination fills me up without requiring me to rethink every single meal. Slowly, a new way of eating took shape. And with it, the day started to feel workable again. So often, the body is the first to experiment, the first to get tired, the first to find the path of least resistance — and only afterward does the mind catch up and realize: I’ve already started adapting to this place.

### After Language Stopped Working, I Thought I’d Be Free

I used to have a romanticized fantasy about “living somewhere where you can’t understand the language.” I always felt overloaded with information in my mother tongue environment, and imagined that not understanding what people said would relieve that overload.

In a familiar environment, we take in far more information than language alone carries. A tone of voice, a pause mid-sentence, a group of people suddenly laughing, even a glance — any of it can become a signal that needs decoding. Most of the time, we’re not even aware we’re processing all this. The body has simply learned to catch it first, judge it, and decide how to respond.

Not understanding the language seemed like it could switch this whole system off. Can’t understand it, so no need to interpret it. No need to interpret it, so no need to carry whatever comes after understanding. I used to think that was a kind of freedom. But now that I’m actually here, I’ve found it’s only half true.

Not understanding the language really did cut off a lot of information — but it didn’t reduce my perception of the environment at all. If anything, once language stopped working, I started relying even more on expressions, gestures, sound, distance, movement — all the details I can’t fully explain. Not knowing what’s happening doesn’t make the body stop being alert. Sometimes it makes it more sensitive.

I started to realize that what we call “reading the room” isn’t purely a language skill. Language is just one way of understanding an environment. Even with language removed, the body still tries to figure out where it stands, what the people around it are doing, what it should do next. It’s very hard not to understand your own environment. Even when the mind decides to give up on interpreting, the body keeps watching.

And the trouble with language doesn’t stop there. When you become someone operating in a different language, things that used to be automatic suddenly need conscious processing again. Something trivial in your mother tongue — especially when the environment itself isn’t even your second language — can suddenly become a real hassle. The cognitive path it takes to get something done grows longer.

Language isn’t just a tool for transmitting information. A fluent language is itself a piece of cognitive infrastructure. It lets a huge number of things that would otherwise require conscious effort run almost automatically. And when that fluency is gone, the cognitive costs that used to be hidden by automation resurface.

### The Body That Can’t Blend In

In Chiang Mai, there was a feeling I used to love: I could stay silent. Not understanding the language actually gave me a sense of invisibility. When I wasn’t speaking, I could almost opt out of the environment entirely, without needing to explain myself.

Coming to Barcelona, I assumed I’d have that same freedom. And in some sense, I do. Most of the time, people on the street don’t pay any particular attention to me. Everyone has their own destination, their own phone, their own friends, their own life. Where a stranger comes from usually doesn’t matter much to another stranger.

But my own sense of it is a little different. I notice, instantly, that I’m a minority here. Walking down the street, hearing the language around me, seeing the crowd — I know I don’t belong to the majority this place counts as its own.

It’s a strange thing, because it doesn’t necessarily correspond to anyone actually looking at me. People may not be watching me at all — but I know I’m a minority. That’s when I started to realize “blending into a life” might have two layers. One is objective invisibility: no one knows you, no one pays you particular attention, you can walk down a street just like any other passerby. The other is subjective invisibility: you yourself stop constantly noticing your own difference.

Objective invisibility is when other people simply stop noticing you; subjective invisibility is when your own body stops treating everything around you as a signal demanding constant vigilance. when you see a crowd and no longer instinctively measure the distance between yourself and the majority. I can’t do that second kind of invisibility yet. The language on the street, the makeup of the crowd — my body still processes all of it as information.

Maybe adapting to a new world is just this happening slowly. First, the body lives there: finding a route that works, finding food you can stomach, finding a time and space where you can sleep peacefully, finding a way to be around strangers. And then one day, looking back, you realize that the things you once had to consciously judge and adjust have quietly slipped back out of awareness and become part of life itself.
